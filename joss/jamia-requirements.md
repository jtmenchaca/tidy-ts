Article types and word counts

JAMIA word limits exclude materials in Acknowledgments and in References sections. Supplemental materials such as additional tables, figures, data sets and source code can be included for online only publication. For all articles, authors are required to submit related published materials (including articles published in conference proceedings) to allow reviewers to assess the degree of overlap. These materials can be entered as appendices "for review only".

Please note that all submissions should be double-spaced.

Research and Applications

Research and Applications articles describe original work in the formulation, implementation, or evaluation of informatics-based studies and investigations. The articles do not need to be limited to hypothesis-driven research, and they can, for example, report on an innovative application of information technology, the detailed description of a new methodology, or the formulation and formative evaluation of a new model. The structured abstract should contain the headings: Objective, Materials and Methods, Results, Discussion, and Conclusion. The main text should, in addition to the sections corresponding to these headings, include a section describing Background and Significance.

Word count: up to 4000 words.
Structured abstract: up to 250 words.
Tables: up to 4.
Figures: up to 6.
References: unlimited.

Extra guidance:

No em-dashes, semicolons, colons used for elaboration, or "It's not X. It's Y" constructions
Each sentence should flow logically from the prior one
Claims should be followed by substantiation that clearly serves the claim (no irrelevant detail)
Concise, accessible language (no unnecessary jargon)
Interpret findings rather than just reporting them

Read no more than 2 lines at a time.

For each line, list every phrase and evaluate it against these questions:

Does a non-technical but engaged JAMIA reader know what this means?
Does this phrase introduce a term that hasn't been explained?
Is this the language we've been using elsewhere in the paper, or does it introduce a synonym or jargon version of something we already said plainly?
Does this phrase say something the previous sentence already said?
Is this the right word, or is it a word that sounds precise but means something slightly different from what we intend?
After the phrase-by-phrase review, generate at least 3 questions a non-technical but engaged reader might ask after reading these lines. Not softballs — genuine confusion or skepticism.

For each question, decide whether a change is indicated. Default to yes. The burden is on keeping the text as-is, not on changing it. If a reader would be confused, that's a problem even if the information is technically available elsewhere.

State proposed changes and wait for approval before editing.

After editing, do not move on. Wait for explicit approval.

After completing a subsection, reread the full subsection and verify:

Each sentence flows logically from the prior one
No jargon was introduced that wasn't there before
The heading matches the content
The subsection connects to the one before and after it
Never dismiss a reader question by saying "the context grounds it" or "explained earlier." If the line doesn't stand on its own, fix it.
