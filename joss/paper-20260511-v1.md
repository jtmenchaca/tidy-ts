## Abstract

### Objective

Clinical data analyses often require many table operations: selecting columns, joining patient and encounter records, reshaping laboratory and vital-sign data, converting strings to numbers or dates, and handling missing values. Small mistakes in these steps can produce incorrect results, especially when errors are not detected until late in the workflow or continue without warning. This study evaluates whether a type-safe dataframe framework can detect common clinical data transformation errors before execution.

### Materials and Methods

We developed Tidy-TS, a TypeScript framework for dataframe transformation, statistical analysis, visualization, asynchronous data processing, and schema-validated data ingestion. Tidy-TS represents each dataframe row as a TypeScript type and updates that type as the dataframe changes. Operations such as `select`, `drop`, `mutate`, `rename`, `summarize`, joins, pivots, row binding, and missing-data handling update the known shape of the data. We evaluated the framework with three test artifacts: a compile-time guarantee audit, runtime boundary-validation tests, and a cross-ecosystem comparison suite using clinical fixtures representing patients, encounters, laboratory results, medications, and vital signs. Equivalent probes were implemented in Tidy-TS, Python/pandas, and R/tidyverse. Each outcome was classified as a compile-time error, runtime error, runtime warning, or silent continuation. Tidy-TS also includes statistical routines, I/O support, asynchronous operations, visualization support, columnar storage, and WebAssembly acceleration. 

### Results

The compile-time audit verified exact output types for common dataframe operations, including unchanged row shape after filtering and sorting, narrowed schemas after selecting or dropping columns, added and replaced columns after mutation, new schemas after summarization and reshaping, join-introduced missing values, row-binding unions, grouping state, and null/undefined narrowing. Invalid operations, including stale column references, invalid join keys, unsafe use of nullable values, invalid post-aggregation references, and shape-incompatible append/prepend operations, were rejected by the TypeScript compiler. Runtime boundary tests confirmed that schema-backed dataframe construction rejected wrong scalar types, missing required fields, null values in non-nullable fields, and invalid categorical values, while preserving valid nullable schemas. Cross-ecosystem probes showed that many errors rejected before execution by Tidy-TS were detected only at runtime in pandas or tidyverse, and that missing values, invalid comparisons, type coercions, join-introduced missingness, and window-function boundary values often continued silently in dynamic workflows. Type-checker performance testing showed that the compile-time audit checked in approximately 1.2 seconds, schema-width examples from 5 to 100 columns checked in approximately 1.2–2.4 seconds, and deeply chained mutation pipelines reached a TypeScript depth limit between 33 and 35 consecutive transformations.

### Conclusion

Tidy-TS demonstrates that common clinical dataframe errors can be detected earlier by making table shape, column types, join effects, and missing values visible to the programming language before code runs. This approach does not replace runtime validation or statistical review, but it shifts a meaningful class of clinical data transformation errors from late detection or silent continuation to earlier detection during development.

## Background and Significance

### Clinical Data Pipelines Are Error-Prone by Nature

Clinical data work depends on tables. Patient demographics, encounters, diagnoses, medications, laboratory results, vital signs, claims, and device streams are usually transformed through a series of table operations before they are analyzed. A typical workflow may read data from files or databases, select a subset of columns, join several tables, convert strings into numbers or dates, reshape long data into wide form, group records by patient or encounter, summarize measurements, and then pass the result into a statistical model or dashboard.

These steps are routine, but they are also a well-documented source of error. Systematic reviews of electronic health record (EHR) data quality consistently identify completeness, correctness, and concordance as the most commonly assessed — and most frequently deficient — quality dimensions.[1][2][3] A systematic review and meta-analysis of data processing error rates in clinical research found rates ranging from 0.02% to 27.8% depending on the method, with some approaches producing error rates high enough to impact decisions made using the data and necessitate increases in sample sizes to preserve statistical power.[4] In a study of Medicaid administrative data, Zhang and Koru identified five major categories of data defects — missingness, incorrectness, syntax violations, semantic violations, and duplicity — and detected over 3 million defects across 2.23 million rows.[5] Quindroit et al. developed a taxonomy identifying 53 distinct data quality problems in hospital databases, classified across six levels of granularity.[6] In a quality improvement study of clinical research datasets, 69% of datasets submitted for statistical analysis contained two or more defects at baseline, with common problems including missing values, miscoded values, implausible values, and inconsistencies with codebooks.[7]

These defects are not confined to data entry. The extract-transform-load (ETL) processes that populate clinical research databases introduce their own errors. Denney et al. validated a clinical data warehouse ETL process and found 19 discordant observations out of 498 (3.8%), attributable to design decision differences, timing mismatches, and user interface settings.[8] Hartzema et al. documented specific ETL errors in the OMOP Common Data Model, including zip codes incorrectly loaded, drug quantities rounded, drug exposure length incorrectly calculated, and condition length incorrectly programmed.[9] Tahar et al. confirmed that ETL processes for rare disease data influence the quality of transformed data and found discrepancies between pre- and post-ETL data quality metrics.[10] In a large pediatric clinical data research network, 2,182 data quality issues were identified across nine data submission cycles, with the most frequent types being missing data and outliers, and 9% of issues traced to ETL errors.[11] A simulation study of a French clinical data warehouse estimated that depending on the pattern of data loss through the processing infrastructure, only 42–76% of patients in a breast cancer cohort would retain all data elements necessary to reconstruct a care pathway.[12]

### Preprocessing Choices Dominate Outcome Variability

The clinical significance of data transformation errors extends beyond data quality metrics. A multiverse analysis by Veltri (2026) across 180 analytical pathways in simulated RCT data found that preprocessing decisions — outlier handling, missing-data imputation, and scale transformation — explained 76.9%–99.8% of the total variance in estimated treatment effects, while model choice explained only 0.1%–7.5%.[13] Specific pipelines that standardized or log-transformed variables shrank effect estimates by more than 90% relative to the raw-data baseline, while others inflated effects by an order of magnitude. This finding demonstrates that the kinds of data transformation steps targeted by this study are not minor housekeeping but the dominant determinant of study conclusions.

Van Essen et al. (2025) provided direct empirical confirmation: two Dutch EHR research databases derived from the same eight general practices through different ETL pipelines showed significant differences in patient counts, contact numbers, prescriptions, and episodes, despite identical source data.[14] The implication is that transformation choices — not source data differences — can produce materially different research datasets.

### Silent Continuation Is the Most Dangerous Failure Mode

Not all data errors are equal in their consequences. Some errors cause a program to stop with a clear message. Others produce warnings. But the most dangerous class of error is silent continuation: the program runs to completion and produces a plausible result that may be wrong. Silent continuation is particularly risky in clinical data workflows because an apparently valid table may be used downstream for cohort definition, quality measurement, modeling, visualization, or decision-support evaluation.

Sauer et al. describe six common pitfalls in EHR data science — including imprecise variable definitions and sample selection bias — and note that these errors often go undetected because the analysis produces an apparently valid output.[7] The missing data literature further illustrates this risk. A survey of UK primary care EHR studies found that many studies fail to follow published best practice for handling missing data, often relying on flawed methods such as the missing indicator method that silently introduce bias.[16] Buyse et al. demonstrated that while random errors require substantial prevalence before affecting trial outcomes, even a small amount of systematic error can severely bias estimated treatment effects — a finding that underscores the danger of errors that are introduced silently and propagate systematically through a pipeline.[17] Shun-Shin and Francis showed that reclassification of just 5 out of 200 patients (2.5%) was sufficient to turn a neutral study positive, and that larger studies were proportionately more vulnerable — demonstrating that even small systematic perturbations, of the kind that silent data errors can introduce, can flip study conclusions.[18]

The consequences of silent data errors extend beyond research validity to clinical care. Kramer et al. directly measured the impact of data quality defects on clinical decisions in the ICU and found that lower data validity increased the likelihood of medication prescriptions and invasive procedures across all five clinical scenarios studied, even without practitioners' awareness.[19] Wright et al. analyzed 68 clinical decision support (CDS) alert malfunctions across 14 US sites and found that build errors, conceptualization errors, and the introduction of new concepts or terms were the most frequent causes, with 93% of Chief Medical Information Officers reporting at least one malfunction.[20] Stone described two cases where CDS malfunctions were caused by changes in external data systems — a pharmacy system recategorization and a laboratory instrument change — that silently altered the data feeding into decision rules.[21] A scoping review by Thayer et al. identified 130 CDS malfunctions across 28 studies, noting that only 22% described the potential impact on patient care.[22] These findings demonstrate that the silent continuation problem is not hypothetical: data transformation errors in clinical systems have caused real CDS malfunctions that persisted undetected.

The broader reproducibility literature reinforces this concern. Glasziou et al. estimated that at least 50% of research reports were sufficiently poor or incomplete as to make them unusable, representing a waste of tens of billions of pounds.[35] The FAIRSCAPE framework for FAIR and reproducible biomedical analytics emphasizes that analysis code in medical research is rarely made available and too often only partly reproducible, recommending transparent provenance tracking, systematic code review, and accessible code repositories.[24] These findings suggest that the problem is not only that errors occur, but that the tools and workflows in common use do not make errors visible early enough to prevent downstream consequences.

### Existing Approaches Focus on Runtime Assessment, Not Compile-Time Prevention

A substantial body of work addresses clinical data quality through runtime assessment frameworks, data quality indicators, and post-hoc validation tools. Schmidt et al. developed an R-based data quality framework with 34 indicators covering integrity, completeness, consistency, and accuracy for observational health research.[25] Reproducible analysis pipelines using bioinformatics workflow managers have been developed to standardize data quality analysis tools for deployment across clinical data research network sites.[26] Automated frameworks for medical data curation have been developed to detect inconsistencies, missing values, outliers, and duplicates at runtime.[1] Provenance-tracking frameworks have been developed to trace data through ETL processes and identify error patterns, but these operate retrospectively on data that has already been transformed.[27] Semantic technologies — including controlled vocabularies, ontologies, and natural language processing — have been applied to improve EHR data quality across dimensions such as conformance, portability, and usability.[7]

These approaches are valuable but share a common characteristic: they operate on data after it has been produced. They assess quality retrospectively rather than preventing transformation errors prospectively. The distinction matters because many clinical data errors are not problems with the source data itself but with the transformations applied to it — a misspelled column name, a stale reference after a reshape, a type mismatch after a conversion, or a join that silently introduces missing values into a previously complete field.

Workflow managers such as Snakemake and Nextflow address a related but different problem: reproducibility and scalability of multi-step computational pipelines.[26] They ensure that analysis steps are executed in the correct order, with correct dependencies, and can be re-run on new data. However, they do not inspect the internal structure of dataframe transformations within each step. A Snakemake rule that runs a Python script will faithfully re-execute that script, but it cannot detect that the script refers to a column that no longer exists after a prior reshape.

Cobo et al. (2026) proposed FAIR4prep, a JSON-LD schema for standardized reporting of preprocessing steps in clinical informatics, arguing that preprocessing pipelines are not systematically reported in a consistent, standardized way.[28] This addresses documentation of preprocessing decisions — an important complement to the present work, which addresses enforcement of structural correctness within those preprocessing steps.

Principles for EHR data quality assessment and reuse have been articulated by Madandola et al. and Weiskopf and Weng, but these focus on organizational practices — documentation, version control, code review — rather than on language-level enforcement of data contracts.[29][30] The gap is not in awareness of the problem but in the availability of tools that can catch transformation errors before code runs.

### Static Type Systems Prevent Specific Classes of Defects

The approach evaluated in this study relies on a specific mechanism: static type checking, in which a programming language verifies properties of code before execution. The theoretical foundation for this mechanism is well established. Sound static type systems are designed to guarantee the absence of certain classes of runtime errors by construction, ensuring that well-typed programs cannot produce type-level faults during execution.[36][37]

Empirical evidence supports the practical effectiveness of this mechanism for specific defect classes. In a controlled experiment, Prechelt and Tichy (1998) found that procedure argument type checking in ANSI C significantly reduced delivered interface defects compared to untyped K&R C, and that after familiarization, programmers using the typed language removed defects faster and were more productive.[38] Gao, Bird, and Barr (2017) replayed public, checked-in JavaScript bugs through Flow and TypeScript type checkers and found that approximately 15% were detectable by static typing — a figure the authors explicitly frame as a lower bound, since the analyzed bugs had already survived code review and testing.[39] Khan et al. (2021) conducted an analogous defect-replay study for Python and found that mypy-style static checking could likely avoid approximately 15% of corrective defects and 11% of all defects, with null handling, dynamic attribute initialization, and reference redefinition among the most common type-related defect categories.[40] These defect categories — null misuse, attribute errors, and type confusion — correspond closely to the error classes that Tidy-TS is designed to detect in dataframe pipelines: nullable value misuse, stale column references, and type mismatches after transformation.

However, the evidence also establishes clear boundaries on what static typing can and cannot achieve. Hanenberg et al. (2014) found that static types improved maintainability for tasks involving understanding undocumented code and fixing type errors, but did not help with semantic errors — a distinction directly relevant to this study, which claims detection of structural transformation errors but not clinical or statistical correctness.[41] Bogner and Merkel (2022) compared TypeScript and JavaScript projects on GitHub and found that TypeScript projects showed better code quality and understandability, but not significantly lower bug proneness or faster bug resolution.[42] Berger et al. (2019) reanalyzed a large-scale GitHub mining study of programming languages and code quality and found that language-defect associations, while statistically significant, were small in practical magnitude and methodologically fragile — cautioning against broad claims that any single language feature dramatically reduces overall defect rates.[43] Tang, Alimadadi, and Sumner (2026) found that while TypeScript has reduced traditional runtime and type errors in its ecosystem, it has shifted fragility toward tooling, configuration, API misuse, and asynchronous error-handling issues, suggesting that type safety addresses one layer of defects without eliminating others.[44]

Taken together, this evidence supports a specific and defensible claim: static typing reliably prevents or exposes particular classes of defects — interface mismatches, null and type misuse, stale references, and type-confusion errors — with the strongest quantified estimates placing the preventable share of public defects at approximately 15% for JavaScript/TypeScript and Python.[39][40] Broader claims that static typing generally reduces production bug rates are supported by some observational evidence but are methodologically contested and not cleanly causal.[43] The present study does not make such broad claims. Instead, it applies the narrower, well-supported mechanism — compile-time detection of structural type errors — to the specific domain of clinical dataframe transformations, where null misuse, column reference errors, and type mismatches are among the most common and consequential failure modes.

### TypeScript Is Already Widely Used in Clinical Applications

Most clinical data analysis is performed in dynamic dataframe environments such as Python/pandas or R/tidyverse. These tools are powerful and widely used, but many errors are discovered only when code runs. Some failures are clear because the program stops with an error. Others are harder to detect because the program continues and produces a plausible result.

Meanwhile, a substantial and growing portion of clinical data work occurs in TypeScript. Web dashboards, data collection tools, APIs, decision-support prototypes, registry interfaces, and real-time clinical systems often contain data transformation logic outside traditional statistical environments. Gour et al. describe a healthcare interoperability platform built with TypeScript and Deno for FHIR-based patient data integration, deployed in multinational EU projects.[31] Semenov et al. developed an FHIR-based medical data management platform to provide clinical decision support, illustrating the use of web technologies in clinical data systems.[32] FHIR itself — now the consensus-based standard for health data exchange in the United States — is built on JavaScript Object Notation and RESTful APIs, making TypeScript a natural language for clinical data-facing applications. Clinical decision support systems built on web standards increasingly rely on interoperable clinical applications across EHR systems that align directly with the TypeScript ecosystem.[33]

Despite this prevalence, TypeScript has not traditionally been treated as a primary language for statistical data analysis. Clinical applications that receive, transform, and display patient data in TypeScript typically export that data to Python or R for analysis, then re-import results — a boundary crossing that introduces its own opportunities for error and makes end-to-end type safety impossible.

### A Type-Safe Dataframe Framework Can Address This Gap

The central question in this study is whether some of these errors can be detected earlier. Rather than waiting until code runs, a programming language can sometimes check that an operation is valid before execution. This requires the language to know what columns exist, what types those columns contain, and whether values may be missing. In ordinary dataframe use, this information is usually known by the analyst but not represented in a way that the programming environment can enforce.

Software engineering best practices — including unit testing, clean code, and systematic validation — have been recommended for biomedical software development.[15] Tidy-TS extends this logic from general software quality to the specific domain of dataframe transformations. Its key design choice is that dataframe operations update the known shape of the data as the pipeline changes. After a column is dropped, later code cannot refer to it. After a left join, fields from the right table are treated as possibly missing. After converting a string to a number in a way that can fail, the result is treated as numeric or null until the null case is handled.

This approach is complementary to existing data quality frameworks. Runtime assessment tools check whether data meet quality standards after production.[1][25] Workflow managers ensure reproducible execution of pipeline steps.[26] Provenance-tracking systems trace data through ETL processes to identify error patterns.[27] Preprocessing reporting schemas document transformation decisions for reproducibility.[28] Code review and documentation practices improve human oversight.[24][29] Tidy-TS adds a different layer: language-level enforcement of data contracts within the transformation pipeline itself, catching structural errors — misspelled columns, stale references, unhandled missingness, type mismatches — before code runs.

This study evaluates Tidy-TS as an approach to safer clinical dataframe programming. The goal is not to show that TypeScript is generally superior to Python or R for clinical analysis. The goal is narrower: to determine whether a type-aware dataframe design can detect a meaningful class of clinical data transformation errors earlier than conventional dynamic dataframe workflows, and to characterize the practical limits of this approach.

## Objective

The objective of this study was to develop and evaluate a type-safe dataframe framework for clinical data workflows, with emphasis on detecting common table transformation errors before execution.

The study focused on four practical questions:

1. Can a dataframe library track column names, column types, missing values, and join effects across common clinical data transformations?
2. Can the programming environment reject invalid downstream operations before the code runs?
3. How do equivalent clinical data errors behave in Tidy-TS, Python/pandas, and R/tidyverse?
4. What are the practical limits of this approach, including runtime validation needs, TypeScript escape hatches, and type-checker performance?

## Materials and Methods

### System Design

Tidy-TS is a TypeScript framework for working with tabular data. It provides a dataframe abstraction for arrays of objects, which are common in JavaScript and TypeScript applications. Each dataframe has a row type that describes the columns available in that dataframe and the type of value in each column.

For example, a dataframe with patient identifiers, names, and ages has a row type containing those fields. When an operation changes the dataframe, the row type changes as well. Selecting columns narrows the row type. Dropping columns removes fields. Mutating data adds or replaces fields. Grouping and summarizing produce a new row type. Joins combine row types and mark fields as possibly missing when the join can introduce missing values.

The framework includes dataframe operations inspired by common R and Python workflows, including filtering, sorting, selecting, dropping, renaming, mutating, grouping, summarizing, joining, pivoting, binding rows, filling missing values, and extracting columns. It also includes statistical functions, hypothesis tests, visualization support, asynchronous transformations, schema-backed data ingestion, and support for browser, server, and notebook environments. 

### Schema Validation at Data Boundaries

Static checking can only reason about data after the program has a trustworthy description of the input. For external data, Tidy-TS supports runtime schema validation using Zod. A schema can be supplied when creating a dataframe. The schema checks the input rows at runtime and produces a dataframe whose TypeScript row type is inferred from the schema.

This separates two safety problems. At the boundary, runtime validation checks whether incoming data match the expected structure. Inside the pipeline, TypeScript checks whether later transformations are valid given the known structure of the dataframe.

### Compile-Time Guarantee Audit

We created a compile-time audit file to test the static behavior of the dataframe API. This file type-checks but is not intended to run. It uses type-level assertions to verify the exact output type of dataframe operations. It also uses `@ts-expect-error` comments to verify that invalid programs are rejected.

The audit covers schema-preserving operations, schema-narrowing operations, schema-extending operations, schema-replacing operations, null/undefined introduction, null/undefined removal, grouping state, chained pipelines, schema-derived construction, and documented escape hatches. Examples include verifying that `select` returns only selected columns, `drop` removes dropped columns, `mutate` adds or replaces columns, `summarize` produces a new row type, joins mark unmatched-side fields as possibly missing, and `removeNull` or `replaceNull` narrows nullable fields after explicit handling. 

### Runtime Boundary Tests

We created runtime validation tests as a companion to the compile-time audit. These tests confirm behavior that cannot be proven by static types alone. They test schema rejection for wrong scalar types, missing required fields, null values in non-nullable fields, and invalid categorical values. They also test valid schema acceptance, nullable value preservation, append/prepend behavior, fill-forward/fill-backward edge cases, empty dataframe behavior, and runtime behavior for null removal and replacement. 

### Cross-Ecosystem Error Comparison

We created a comparison suite using clinical-style test fixtures. The fixtures include patients, encounters, laboratory results, medications, and vital signs. Each test represents a common clinical data transformation mistake, such as a misspelled patient identifier, a stale encounter column after summarization, a pivoted vital-sign column that does not exist, arithmetic on a nullable laboratory value, a join that introduces missing data, or an invalid categorical value.

Equivalent probes were implemented in Tidy-TS, Python/pandas, and R/tidyverse. Each probe was classified by outcome:

- **Compile-time error:** rejected before execution.
- **Runtime error:** program stops when the line executes.
- **Runtime warning:** program warns but continues.
- **Silent continuation:** program continues without error or warning, producing a result that may conflict with the analyst’s intended assumption.

The comparison suite groups errors into categories such as column and schema reference errors, type safety errors, null and missing data errors, and join safety errors. The example comparison file shows clinical fixtures and tests for patients, labs, encounters, and vitals, including compile-time tests for Tidy-TS and runtime probes for Tidy-TS, Python, and R. 

### Severity Classification

Each probe was classified by potential downstream consequence using a two-axis framework that separates potential severity from detection phase, following the structural logic of Failure Modes and Effects Analysis (FMEA), in which severity is scored independent of occurrence and detectability based solely on the consequence if the failure reaches the output.[45][46] The O'Kane A-score/P-score system for laboratory error grading operationalizes the same principle: the P-score reflects "the worst case possible outcome that might have resulted," independent of whether the error was actually intercepted.[47][48] This separation avoids circular reasoning in which an error is classified as unimportant because it is caught, when in fact it is caught because it would be important.

**Axis 1 — Potential severity** was assigned to each probe independent of detection outcome in any ecosystem, based on the following criteria:

**High** (all three required): (1) the error alters a computed value, cohort membership, or categorical assignment that would enter an analytic endpoint, quality measure, or decision rule; (2) the alteration propagates systematically — affecting every row meeting a structurally defined condition in the same direction, or systematically including/excluding patients from a cohort, rather than introducing random noise; and (3) the corrupted output remains plausible absent domain-specific validation — it would pass a generic sanity check (non-null, non-zero, within data-type range) even if it might not survive expert review. The systematic-vs-random distinction follows Buyse et al., who showed that systematic data errors bias treatment-effect estimates even at low prevalence, while random errors attenuate effects without reversing them.[17]

**Moderate** (both required): (1) the error corrupts an intermediate column, table, or derived variable that feeds into downstream operations but is not itself a final analytic endpoint; and (2) the error does NOT meet all three High criteria — typically because the corruption does not propagate systematically, does not reach an endpoint in the current pipeline, or produces output with detectable symptoms (visible NaN, unexpected dtype, implausible row count) before reaching an endpoint. Moderate errors are dangerous through composition: they create corrupted intermediates that become High severity if the pipeline is extended, reused, or composed with other steps.

**Low** (any one sufficient): (1) the error produces obviously implausible output that no analyst would proceed with without investigation (zero rows, all-NaN column, negative count); (2) the error affects only non-analytic properties such as display formatting, column ordering, or error-message quality; or (3) the error is self-correcting — overwritten or discarded by a subsequent mandatory pipeline step.

**Axis 2 — Detection phase** was recorded separately for each ecosystem using the same four-level classification as Table 1: compile-time error, runtime error, runtime warning, or silent continuation. Classification was performed per-probe (not per-ecosystem): each probe is tiered by the worst-case consequence across ecosystems that permit the error to reach that stage. Two probes from the original 67 were removed during severity review because they tested edge-case semantics of empty-dataframe aggregation (design choices about identity values) rather than errors that a framework should catch, yielding 65 probes in the severity analysis.

### Type-Checker Performance Measurement

Because the approach relies on TypeScript’s type checker, we measured the cost of checking representative files. Measurements included the full compile-time audit, pipelines with varying schema width, and pipelines with varying chain depth. Chain-depth tests were also used to identify the point at which deeply repeated transformations reach TypeScript’s type-instantiation limit.

### Statistical Function Validation

Tidy-TS includes statistical functions and hypothesis tests. These are separate from the dataframe safety evaluation but are part of the broader framework. Statistical tests are validated against R using randomized test suites that compare test statistics and p-values within a tolerance of 1e-6.

## Results

### Compile-Time Schema Tracking

The compile-time audit showed that Tidy-TS preserved or changed dataframe row types according to the expected behavior of each operation.

Operations that should not change columns, such as filtering, sorting, slicing, shuffling, reordering, and interpolation, preserved the row type. Operations that remove columns, such as `select`, `drop`, and `distinct`, produced narrower row types. Operations that add or replace columns, such as `mutate` and `rename`, produced updated row types. Operations that produce a new table shape, such as `summarize`, `count`, `pivotWider`, `pivotLonger`, and `unnest`, produced new row types.

The audit also verified that joins changed the row type according to join semantics. Inner joins preserved matched fields without adding missingness. Left joins marked right-side fields as possibly undefined. Right joins marked left-side fields as possibly undefined. Outer joins marked non-key fields from both sides as possibly undefined. Joins with column-name collisions exposed suffixed column names and rejected later references to the original ambiguous name.

Missingness was also tracked. Functions such as `lag` introduced `undefined`. Row binding with different schemas marked fields that were not present in all inputs as possibly undefined. Operations such as `removeNull`, `replaceNull`, and `removeUndefined` narrowed types after explicit handling. Fill-forward and fill-backward preserved nullability because leading or trailing missing values may remain. 

### Rejected Invalid Programs

The audit confirmed that invalid dataframe programs were rejected before execution. Examples included selecting or dropping nonexistent columns, using a dropped column after selection, using an old column name after renaming, referencing pre-aggregation columns after summarization, using pivot-consumed columns after reshaping, joining on invalid keys, using nullable values in arithmetic without first handling nulls, calling methods on possibly missing join fields, and appending or prepending rows with missing or incorrectly typed fields. 

### Runtime Boundary Validation

Runtime validation tests confirmed that schema-backed dataframe construction rejected malformed input. Zod schemas rejected rows with wrong scalar types, missing required fields, null values in non-nullable fields, and invalid categorical values. Valid rows were accepted, and nullable schemas preserved null values when allowed.

Runtime tests also confirmed behavior for missing-data edge cases. Fill-forward filled interior and trailing missing values when a prior value existed but left leading nulls unchanged. Fill-backward filled leading missing values when a later value existed but left trailing nulls unchanged. These results support the static decision to preserve nullability after fill-forward and fill-backward rather than claiming all missing values were removed. 

### Cross-Ecosystem Comparison

The comparison suite showed that Tidy-TS detected many common clinical dataframe errors before execution. In the column and schema reference category, Tidy-TS rejected misspelled columns, nonexistent predicate fields, stale names after renaming, dropped columns, post-summarization references, and invalid post-pivot references at compile time. Equivalent pandas and tidyverse programs often failed at runtime, while some schema-narrowing differences continued silently when the dynamic workflow retained columns that the analyst may have assumed were removed. 

In the type safety category, Tidy-TS rejected arithmetic on string columns, numeric aggregation over string columns, comparisons between incompatible values, arithmetic after failed conversion, mixed-type transformations used as numeric values, temporal misuse, numeric functions applied to categorical fields, and invalid categorical comparisons. Equivalent pandas and tidyverse behavior varied by operation: some cases raised runtime errors, some produced warnings, and others continued silently through coercion, empty filters, or missing-value propagation. 

In the null and missing data category, Tidy-TS rejected unsafe method calls, arithmetic, comparisons, aggregations, sorting, lagged-value arithmetic, and pivot-derived arithmetic when values could be null or undefined. Dynamic workflows frequently continued silently, including cases involving NaN/NA propagation, skipped missing values in aggregation, missing boundary values introduced by window functions, and implicit ordering of missing values during sorting. 

In the join safety category, Tidy-TS rejected invalid join keys, misspelled keys, missing post-join columns, unsafe use of join-introduced missing values, comparisons on possibly missing fields, and stale references after suffixing colliding column names. In pandas and tidyverse, invalid join keys generally raised runtime errors, while missingness introduced by left joins frequently continued silently unless explicitly handled.

### Severity-Weighted Detection Analysis

The two-axis classification (Table 2) reveals that the detection gap between Tidy-TS and dynamic ecosystems is concentrated in the most consequential error tier. Of the 30 probes classified as High potential severity, 0 continued silently in Tidy-TS (28 rejected at compile time, 2 caught by runtime schema validation), compared with 30 of 30 (100%) continuing silently in pandas and 27 of 30 (90%) continuing silently in tidyverse. The 2 High-severity probes detected at Tidy-TS runtime involve schema violations in external data, where runtime validation is the correct detection mechanism because the error is in the data, not the code.

The concentration of High-severity probes in the null and missing data category (12 of 30) reflects a specific clinical risk: when a nullable laboratory value silently propagates through an aggregation, a comparison silently excludes null rows, or a join-introduced missing value enters arithmetic, the resulting table is plausible but systematically biased. All 12 of these probes continued silently in both pandas and tidyverse. This pattern — plausible output with systematic bias — corresponds directly to the error mode that Buyse et al. [17] identified as most dangerous for clinical trial outcomes.

At Moderate severity, the gap narrows: pandas caught 8 of 16 at runtime and tidyverse caught 11 of 16 (9 errors, 2 warnings), while Tidy-TS caught 15 of 16 at compile time. At Low severity, dynamic ecosystems caught the majority at runtime (pandas 16 of 19, tidyverse 15 of 19), consistent with the observation that Low-severity errors tend to be the ones that language designers made into hard failures — a misspelled column name raises a KeyError in pandas and a tidyselect error in R. Tidy-TS caught all 19 at compile time, shifting detection earlier but not changing the practical outcome for errors that would have been caught regardless.

Pandas produced zero warnings across all 65 probes, exhibiting binary behavior (runtime error or silent continuation) for these probe types. R produced 4 warnings, all in the Moderate or High tiers — an intermediate detection state whose practical value depends on workflow context, since warnings in scripted batch pipelines are easily missed.

### Type-Checker Performance

The type-checker performance script showed that the compile-time audit checked in approximately 1.2 seconds. Representative schema-width pipelines from 5 to 100 columns checked in approximately 1.2–2.4 seconds. Deeply chained mutation pipelines showed a practical limit: approximately 33 consecutive `.mutate()` calls checked successfully, while approximately 35 consecutive calls triggered TypeScript’s deep type-instantiation error. This boundary reflects a host-language limit in TypeScript rather than a runtime limitation of dataframe execution.

### Statistical Function Validation

The framework includes statistical hypothesis-testing routines with typed results, including test names, p-values, effect sizes, test statistics, confidence intervals, degrees of freedom, and alternatives. These routines are validated against R using randomized tests with agreement within 1e-6 for test statistics and p-values.

## Discussion

This study shows that many routine dataframe errors in clinical data workflows can be detected before code runs. The key design is simple: the dataframe carries a description of its columns and value types, and each transformation updates that description. If later code refers to a column that no longer exists, uses a value that may be missing, or treats a string as a number, the compiler can reject the code.

This is important because clinical data pipelines often fail in small, ordinary ways. The problem is rarely that an analyst writes a completely invalid program. More often, an assumption becomes stale. A field is renamed. A join changes completeness. A pivot creates some columns but not others. A summary step removes detailed encounter fields. A missing laboratory reference range becomes part of a calculation. These are the kinds of errors Tidy-TS is designed to surface.

The comparison with pandas and tidyverse should be interpreted carefully. Python and R are mature, powerful, and widely used for clinical data analysis. The point is not that these tools are defective. The point is that their default dataframe workflows often detect errors later because the shape and missingness of the dataframe are not checked by the language before execution. Runtime errors are helpful when they stop the program. Silent continuation is more concerning because it can produce a plausible table that enters downstream analysis.

Not all silent continuations are equally dangerous. The severity-weighted analysis (Table 2) classifies each probe by potential downstream consequence using a two-axis framework that separates potential severity from detection phase. Of the 30 probes classified as High potential severity — errors that alter computed values, propagate systematically, and produce plausible output — all 30 continued silently in pandas, 27 of 30 continued silently in tidyverse, and 0 continued silently in Tidy-TS (28 caught at compile time, 2 by runtime schema validation). The concentration of High-severity probes in the null and missing data category (12 of 30) reflects a specific clinical risk: when a nullable laboratory value silently propagates through an aggregation or a join-introduced missing value silently enters a comparison, the resulting table is plausible but systematically biased — exactly the error pattern that Buyse et al. [17] showed can severely bias treatment effect estimates even at low prevalence. The framework's primary safety contribution is not catching more errors in total — dynamic ecosystems catch most Low-severity errors at runtime — but catching High-severity errors that dynamic ecosystems miss entirely.

Tidy-TS also does not eliminate the need for runtime checks. External data must still be validated when loaded. A type-safe pipeline is only as reliable as the data contract at its boundary. For this reason, Tidy-TS combines compile-time checking inside the pipeline with runtime schema validation at input boundaries.

The results also show that TypeScript has practical limits. Type assertions, `any`, and unsafe casts can bypass type checking. Deeply chained transformations can hit the compiler’s type-instantiation limit. These limitations do not invalidate the approach, but they define its scope: Tidy-TS provides strong checks when the typed API is used as designed; it does not make arbitrary TypeScript code sound.

For biomedical informatics, the broader significance is that many clinical data applications already use TypeScript. Web dashboards, data collection tools, APIs, decision-support prototypes, registry interfaces, and real-time clinical systems often contain data transformation logic outside traditional statistical environments. Tidy-TS makes it possible to keep more of that workflow in a single typed environment, reducing the need to export data to a separate analysis language for every transformation and then re-import results into an application.

The framework is deployed in a clinical data pipeline at the University of Utah that maps enterprise data warehouse content into typed analytic datasets for quality improvement and research studies. Across dozens of consumer modules, explicit null narrowing is the most frequently used type-safety operation, consistent with the finding that null and missing data handling represents the largest detection gap between typed and dynamic dataframe workflows. All left-join sites use explicit key specification and immediate post-join handling of introduced nullability, reflecting the join-safety patterns validated in the compile-time audit.

---

## Limitations

This study has several limitations.

First, the comparison suite uses clinical-style fixtures rather than a large collection of real production pipelines. The fixtures are designed to isolate common dataframe failure modes, but they do not measure the frequency of these errors in routine clinical analytics.

Second, the evaluation measures error detection, not downstream patient outcomes, clinical decision quality, or analyst productivity. A future study should evaluate whether this approach reduces debugging time, improves confidence, or prevents errors in real-world clinical research workflows.

Third, TypeScript contains intentional escape hatches. Users can cast through `unknown`, use `any`, or otherwise bypass the checker. Tidy-TS can document and discourage these patterns, but it cannot prevent all unsafe TypeScript code.

Fourth, static checking does not prove clinical correctness. It can verify that a column exists, that a value is numeric, or that missingness was handled. It cannot prove that a cohort definition is appropriate, that a causal model is valid, that a statistical test is justified, or that clinical concepts are correctly specified.

Fifth, runtime validation remains necessary. External files, database results, API responses, and user-provided data may not match expected schemas. Tidy-TS addresses this with Zod-backed validation, but schema design remains the responsibility of the developer.

Sixth, the type-checker performance results are preliminary. They provide useful evidence about feasibility and TypeScript limits, but they should be repeated across machines, TypeScript versions, Deno versions, and larger synthetic pipelines before making broad performance claims.

---

## Conclusion

Tidy-TS provides a type-safe dataframe framework for TypeScript-based clinical data workflows. By tracking dataframe shape, column types, join effects, grouping state, reshaping results, and missingness through ordinary table operations, the framework detects many common clinical data transformation errors before execution. Runtime schema validation complements these checks at data boundaries. The approach does not replace Python, R, clinical review, or statistical validation, but it offers a practical method for shifting a meaningful class of clinical dataframe errors earlier in the development process.
