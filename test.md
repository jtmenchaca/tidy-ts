# Elm trees: a guide for curious readers

Elms are deciduous trees in the genus *Ulmus*, in the elm family (Ulmaceae). They grow across most of the Northern Hemisphere—in temperate forests from North America and Europe into Asia, and in scattered warmer highland regions as far south as Indonesia and parts of Africa.

For centuries, people planted elms in parks, along streets, and in countryside hedgerows because they grow quickly, tolerate pollution, and form graceful, arching canopies. Many towns still remember “elm-lined” avenues. Today, that picture is complicated: Dutch elm disease wiped out most mature elms in Europe and North America in the late 20th century. Breeders have since developed resistant varieties, and elms are slowly returning to landscaping and forestry—but recovery is uneven and still unfolding.

---

## What does an elm look like?

**Leaves** are alternate (one per node along the stem), usually oval with toothed edges—often “double” teeth, like a fine saw on top of a coarser one. Many species have leaves that are slightly lopsided at the base and pointed at the tip.

**Flowers** are small and lack showy petals; wind carries the pollen. Botanists describe the flowers as “perfect” (each flower has both male and female parts).

**Seeds** come in light, papery “helicopters” called samaras—round, green-flushed discs that spin to the ground. A pound of seeds from British elms can hold on the order of 50,000 samaras. The green color helps the seed photosynthesize briefly before the tree’s leaves fully open.

**Shape and size** vary by species. The American elm (*Ulmus americana*) can exceed 30 m (about 100 ft) and often develops a forked trunk with a vase-shaped crown—exactly the profile that made it famous on city streets.

**Bark** can be deeply furrowed (as in slippery elm) or attractively mottled (as in lacebark or Chinese elm). Some species have corky “wings” on young branches.

Elms generally want well-drained soil and tolerate a range of acidity, though they do not thrive in waterlogged ground for long.

---

## How many kinds of elm are there?

Scientists count roughly **30–40 species**, but the number is fuzzy. Elms hybridize easily, and some local populations reproduce mainly by root suckers rather than seed—so “species” blur at the edges. The British botanist Oliver Rackham called *Ulmus* one of the trickiest genera in the UK flora: “species and varieties are a distinction in the human mind rather than a measured degree of genetic variation.”

- **North America** has about eight native species; **Europe** about three.
- **Asia** holds the greatest diversity—around two dozen species.
- Fossil elm leaves date to the Paleocene, more than 60 million years ago.

Elms are distant cousins of nettles, hops, figs, and cannabis—they belong to the same broad group of flowering plants (the urticalean rosids), not to oaks or maples.

## Life in the forest—and in your garden

### How elms reproduce

In the wild, many elms set seed after a warm spring. Seeds ripen quickly—often within weeks—and stay viable only a short time. Gardeners and nurseries also propagate elms by:

- **Root suckers** (common for field elm and hybrids)
- **Cuttings** (hardwood in late winter, softwood in early summer)
- **Grafting** (especially for weeping or dwarf cultivars like ‘Camperdown’)

Because elms cross-pollinate freely, growing from seed can produce unpredictable hybrids. Clones—genetically identical trees—are how we preserve named cultivars.

### Neighbors: insects, birds, and other guests

Elms support a web of life:

- **Aphids and mites** often cause pouch-like or pimple-like galls on leaves—unsightly but usually not fatal.
- The **white-letter hairstreak** butterfly depends on elm; its caterpillars eat elm leaves only.
- **Elm bark beetles** spread Dutch elm disease (see below).
- The **elm leaf beetle**, introduced to North America from Europe, can strip foliage heavily (though trees often survive).
- **Sapsuckers** (woodpeckers) favor young elms.

## Dutch elm disease—and other threats

### Dutch elm disease (DED)

DED is the main reason elms disappeared from many landscapes. Despite the name, Dutch scientists first described the fungus and its beetle vectors in the 1920s—not because the disease originated in the Netherlands.

**What happens:** Tiny **elm bark beetles** bore into bark and carry spores of a fungus (*Ophiostoma* species). The fungus grows in the water-conducting tissue (xylem). The tree tries to block the infection by plugging its own pipes with gum-like growths (tyloses)—which also stops water and nutrients from reaching the crown. The tree wilts and dies.

**Two chapters of the epidemic:**

1. A milder strain (*Ophiostoma ulmi*) reached Europe around 1910 and North America by 1928. It weakened over time in Europe but hit American elm hard.
2. A far more aggressive strain (*Ophiostoma novo-ulmi*) appeared in the mid-20th century and devastated elms in both continents from the 1970s onward.

Most European and North American elm species are highly susceptible. Many Asian species evolved with the fungus and show more resistance.

**Today:** The disease has not “gone away.” Australia and parts of western Canada have largely stayed free thanks to geography and strict quarantine. Everywhere else, mature wild and street elms remain at risk unless they are resistant cultivars or isolated from infected neighbors.

### Elm yellows (elm phloem necrosis)

Spread by leafhoppers or root grafts, this disease attacks the **phloem** (the inner bark layer that carries sugars). It blocks flow much like DED blocks water. There is no cure. It occurs in parts of North America and Europe. Occasionally, cutting an infected tree early and removing debris allows the roots to sprout a new shoot—but that is not a reliable fix.

### Other pests

- **Japanese beetle** (in North America) adds foliage damage where natural predators are missing.
- **Elm zig-zag sawfly** spread in Europe and North America around 2000; impact has been mixed.
- **Slime flux** (wetwood) is a bacterial rot of sapwood that can stain bark—often on weakened or wounded trees.

## Elms in cultivation: rise, fall, and comeback

### The golden age of street elms

From the 18th century into the early 20th century, elms were among the most planted shade trees in Europe and North America.

- **American elm** dominated in the U.S.: fast growth, strong wood, wind resistance, and that iconic vase shape.
- **Wych elm** (*U. glabra*) and **field elm** (*U. minor*) dominated European countryside; their hybrid **Dutch elm** (*U. × hollandica*) was common in towns.
- **English elm** became dense in English hedgerows—sometimes over 1,000 trees per square kilometer.
- **‘Camperdown’ elm**—a weeping form grafted high on a trunk—was prized in large gardens.
- Elms tolerate **salt spray**, so they were planted in coastal towns and shelterbelts.

World War I, nursery disruptions, and the first wave of DED began the decline. After the virulent *O. novo-ulmi* strain spread in the late 1960s, the iconic street elm nearly vanished in many regions.

### Fighting back: resistant trees

Since about 1990, breeding programs in the Netherlands, the U.S., Italy, Spain, and elsewhere have released cultivars with much better DED resistance. Examples include American selections like **‘Valley Forge’** and **‘Jefferson’**, and European hybrids often involving Asian species.

**Caveats worth knowing:**

- A new tree takes decades to reach full size—we still learn how resistant cultivars age in real streets and parks.
- Early “resistant” releases in the 1960s–70s disappointed many buyers; skepticism lingers.
- Some hybrids bred for disease resistance lack the classic vase form of the old American elm.
- A few Dutch clones later proved susceptible to **elm yellows**, not just DED.

Trials such as the **National Elm Trial** in the U.S. (2005 onward) and Butterfly Conservation trials in the UK compare how cultivars perform over years in real conditions.

### Famous survivors

- **Central Park, New York:** Olmsted’s 1860s American elms along the Mall form a cathedral-like canopy. Aggressive pruning and removal of diseased trees, plus replanting with resistant cultivars like **‘Princeton’** and **‘Valley Forge’**, have saved much of the stand.
- **National Mall, Washington, D.C.:** Rows planted in the 1930s were hit by DED from the 1950s on. The National Park Service combined sanitation, fungicide injection, beetle control, and resistant replants to keep the allée alive for more than 80 years.

## What people use elm wood and bark for

Elm wood has **interlocking grain**, so it resists splitting—valued for wagon hubs, chair seats, coffins, and the bodies of Japanese *taiko* drums (where nails must hold without splitting the shell). It bends well; medieval Europeans hollowed trunks for water pipes because elm resists rot when permanently wet (though not when buried in damp soil).

Archers in Europe prized elm for bows when yew was scarce. Ancient Greek records even list elm for chariot wheels.

**Slippery elm** (*U. rubra*): The inner bark is mucilaginous (slimy-soothing when mixed with water) and is sold in the U.S. as an approved nutritional supplement for throat and digestive comfort—traditional use backed by modern regulation as a demulcent.

**Fodder and famine food:** Farmers once lopped elm branches for livestock feed—a practice that continues in parts of the Himalaya and contributes to deforestation there. During Norway’s famine of 1812, people boiled elm bark for sustenance; elm seeds are nutritious (high protein, low fiber by dry weight).

**Vines and elms:** Romans and later Italians trained grapevines up pollarded elms in vineyards—Ovid’s line *ulmus amat vitem* (“the elm loves the vine”) captures a partnership that was both practical and poetic.

**Energy:** Fast-growing resistant clones are being tested in Italy for biomass—some gain more than 2 m of height per year.

**Bonsai:** Chinese elm (*U. parvifolia*) tolerates hard pruning and is a standard choice for bonsai.

## Elms in story, symbol, and politics

### Myth and literature

- In **Greek myth**, elm nymphs (*ptelea*) appear among the hamadryads; Artemis is said to have practiced archery first on an elm, then an oak, then a beast.
- **Homer** has mountain nymphs plant elms on heroes’ tombs; Protesilaus’s grave elms in Thrace were said to wither when they “saw” Troy in the distance—a story of grief that outlasted the hero.
- **Virgil** places a huge Stygian elm in the underworld where empty dreams cling under every leaf—one root of the “elm and death” motif in Western culture.
- Pastoral poetry often pairs **elm and vine** as shade, peace, and masculine/feminine symbols—Shakespeare’s Titania winds Bottom in her arms “as the female Ivy … Enrings the barky fingers of the Elm.”
- **E.M. Forster**’s famous kiss in *Howards End* happens under a great wych elm.

In Norse myth, the first woman **Embla** was made from elm; in Ainu tradition, a chief goddess was born from an elm impregnated by the sky god.

### Politics and place names

- The **Liberty Tree** in Boston—an American white elm—hosted protests against British taxation; when British soldiers cut it in 1775, colonists planted “Liberty Elms” and wore elm emblems.
- **French Revolution** “trees of liberty” were often elms; one survivor, *L’Orme de La Madeleine*, still stood in the 21st century.
- The **cutting of the elm** near Gisors (1188) was a diplomatic quarrel between kings of France and England.
- **Seven Sisters** and **Nine Elms** in London are named for historic elm plantings.

---

## Notable individual trees and art

Many elms became local landmarks by sheer size or age. Artists from **John Constable** to **Vincent van Gogh** (who sometimes mislabeled elms as planes) painted their branching forms and light-filled canopies. Elm appears constantly in 18th–19th century European landscape painting—often the same hybrid *U. × hollandica* that once lined Dutch and English roads.

---

## Conservation

A 1997 European Union project coordinated elm genetic resources across member states, selecting hundreds of clones for disease testing and preservation. Museums, botanic gardens, and citizen groups still hunt for mature survivors and undocumented old cultivars that might carry useful genes.

## If you want to go deeper

- **Richens, R. H.** *Elm* (Cambridge University Press, 1983)—scholarly history, taxonomy, and English regional survey.
- **Heybroek et al.** *Iep of olm* (2009)—Dutch elm culture and resistant cultivars.
- **Wilkinson, G.** *Epitaph for the Elm* (1978)—photographic tribute and introduction.
- **Butterfly Conservation** and **National Elm Trial** reports—for how modern clones perform in the field.

Elms sit at a crossroads: ecological backbone for some butterflies, cultural icon of the street tree era, and cautionary tale about how a single disease can reshape a continent’s treescape. Understanding them means holding all three stories at once—the living tree, the lost canopy, and the slow work of planting again.
