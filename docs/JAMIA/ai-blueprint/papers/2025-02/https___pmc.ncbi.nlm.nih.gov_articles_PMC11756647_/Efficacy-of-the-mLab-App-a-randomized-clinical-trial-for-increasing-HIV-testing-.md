## Abstract

### Objective

To determine the efficacy of the mLab App, a mobile-delivered HIV prevention intervention to increase HIV self-testing in MSM and TGW.

### Materials and methods

This was a randomized (2:2:1) clinical trial of the efficacy the mLab App as compared to standard of care vs mailed home HIV test arm among 525 MSM and TGW aged 18-29 years to increase HIV testing.

### Results

The mLab App arm participants demonstrated an increase from 35.1% reporting HIV testing in the prior 6 months compared to 88.5% at 6 months. In contrast, 28.8% of control participants reported an HIV test at baseline, which only increased to 65.1% at 6 months. In a generalized linear mixed model estimating this change and controlling for multiple observations of participants, this equated to control participants reporting a 61.2% smaller increase in HIV testing relative to mLab participants (_P_ = .001) at 6 months. This difference was maintained at 12 months with control participants reporting an 82.6% smaller increase relative to mLab App participants (_P_ < .001) from baseline to 12 months.

### Discussion and conclusion

Findings suggest that the mLab App is well-supported, evidence-based, behavioral risk-reduction intervention for increasing HIV testing rates as compared to the standard of care, suggesting that this may be a useful behavioral risk-reduction intervention for increasing HIV testing among young MSM.

### Trial registration

This trial was registered with Clinicaltrials.gov [NCT03803683](https://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT03803683).

**Keywords:** mobile health, HIV, self-test, RCT, imaging algorithm

## Background and significance

Approximately 1.2 million people in the United States have HIV and about 13% of those who are infected are unaware of their status.[1](#ocae261-B1) Concurrently, HIV has a disproportionate impact on certain populations in the United States particularly men who have sex with men (MSM) and transgender women (TGW).[2–4](#ocae261-B2) These disparities are further exacerbated in racial and ethnic minorities, with African American/Black and Hispanic/Latino MSM having a 1 in 2 and a 1 in 4 lifetime risk for HIV infection, respectively.[5](#ocae261-B5),[6](#ocae261-B6) The burden is exacerbated in TGW who have an odds ratio 48.8 (95% CI, 21.2-76.3) for being infected with HIV compared with all adults of reproductive age worldwide and does not differ for those in low-income and middle-income countries compared with those in high-income countries.[4](#ocae261-B4)

HIV self-tests (HIVST) and mobile technologies are gateways to reducing onward transmission and the public health burden of HIV.[7–11](#ocae261-B7) HIVST is a key prevention tool but has been met with barriers[12](#ocae261-B12) and also success.[13](#ocae261-B13) Overall HIVST rates are low with several potential contributing factors. Many individuals who are most at risk are unaware of current guidelines for frequent HIV testing (every 3-6 months). Others consumers are concerned about self-testing and finding out the results without knowing how to access confirmatory testing and other resources and finally our research shows that people have difficulty with self-administering the test and interpreting the results. Misinterpretation of the test results is an issue which a mobile app can attempt to address with automated results interpretation and as a result, our study team developed the mLab App to improve the usability of the Oraquick HIV home test.

Specifically, this novel tool affords advantages over existing HIVST options to support uptake by addressing many of the current barriers to self-testing kits through increased privacy and integration with a smartphone to provide automated, immediate reporting and linkage to care in environments with limited resources. In addition, the mLab user interface promotes a holistic diagnostic experience because it provides step-by-step error-checking with clear directions for taking and uploading photo of test results and referral to HIV care in the case of a positive test or PrEP services for those who test HIV-negative. While in principle, rapid tests, such as the OraQuick, seem simple to interpret,[14](#ocae261-B14) it is all-too-often difficult for users to accurately interpret test results with weak positive bands or weak control lines, which we found in our own research on the OraQuick home test.[15](#ocae261-B15) Beyond diagnosis, the mLab App provides information facilitating linkage to care for those who test HIV-positive and educates users on the importance of follow-up testing and prevention services, including PrEP for those who test HIV-negative. The mLab App is an innovative mobile and connected technology that combines HIV prevention information, with text and email notifications to prompt HIV testing, a mobile phone imaging feature of a lateral flow assay (OraQuick), and the automated image processing to provide accessible, objective, secure, and real-time feedback on HIV test results. The mLab App also contains an innovative automated data collection and results reporting feature. The reporting feature relays test results back to the research team in real-time, triggers messages to encourage repeat testing for nonreactive tests or linkage to confirmatory testing and treatment for reactive tests. Finally, the mLab App presents a more accurate measure of testing uptake compared to regular HIVST that have no built-in reporting mechanism.

## Methods

### Study design and participants

This study was a 3-arm randomized clinical trial (RCT). Participants were randomized to either the mLab App intervention including HIV home test kits, standard of care prevention information only, or HIV home test kits.

## Objective

The hypothesis of the study were:

1.  Youth randomized to the mLab App arm (arm 1), as compared to youth in the other 2 arms, will have: (1) increased rates of HIV testing within the first 6 months of the trial; and (2) increased rates of retesting in months 7-12 of the trial.
    
2.  The image processing in the mLab App will: (1) have no statistically significant difference in sensitivity or specificity when compared to confirmatory testing, (2) have greater sensitivity and specificity when compared to the user-submitted self-analyses.
    

The study follows the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) reporting guidelines ([Figure 1](#ocae261-F1)). Columbia University and Lurie Children’s Hospital served as the institutional review boards for the study activities at each of their respective sites. Additionally, since this was determined to be a diagnostic mobile medical app, this was an FDA-regulated study and was assigned IDE No. 18348. An independent data safety and monitoring board was convened and met annually to monitor the study’s progress and any safety concerns. Written or electronic informed consent was obtained for participants.

Recruitment was completed at study sites in New York, New York and Chicago, Illinois. Study enrollment commenced on January 31, 2020 and concluded on August 26, 2022. The final study visit took place on August 18, 2023. Participants were recruited via free and paid online national advertisements promoted on Instagram, Facebook, and Grindr. Participants were also recruited through flyers and the promotion of the study with community partners (eg, community-based organizations) by distributing study-related information flyers to staff and study partners at those sites. Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, participants came in-person to the study site to consent and enroll in the study. In-person research activities were halted in March 2020 while study procedures were revised and approved by the IRB and FDA, allowing the study team to shift study activities online in June 2020.

### Statistical power and sample size calculation

Our sample included 525 young men and TGW, aged 18-29 years.[16](#ocae261-B16) This study used a 2:2:1 RCT design. The study was originally designed and funded as a 1:1 comparison between the mLab App vs standard of care. Our DSMB requested that we add a study arm that serves as an active comparison arm (the mailed HIV home test). The shift to add the extra study arm was not fully powered and it was just to estimate differences if any in the effect size (not efficacy) between an active and inactive control group. We therefore changed this to a 2:2:1 study design and the primary comparison still focused on the mLab App as compared to standard of care.[17](#ocae261-B17)

Eligibility criteria included (1) 18-29 years of age; (2) assigned male sex at the birth of any current gender identification; (3) understand and read English; (4) sexually active and at risk for HIV infection per CDC guidance (eg, MSM or TGW and recent anal sex with men); (5) smartphone ownership; and (6) self-report being HIV-negative or unknown status (verified via HIV testing at the enrollment visit), (7) understand the limitations of the OraQuick home test, (8) had not tested for HIV in the previous 6 months, and (9) were not currently taking PrEP at the time of screening. Participants were excluded if they had a known diagnosis of HIV or if the investigators determined that participation may be detrimental to the participant or to the study.

Compensation for the time to participate in each study visit was provided to participants ($40 initial visit, $55 at 6 months, and $75 at 12 months). Baseline visits were completed in person before the COVID-19 pandemic or via Zoom video conferencing for participants enrolled remotely after the start of the pandemic. Follow-up visits were conducted remotely, and participants completed a survey sent to them electronically.

### mLab App software

A detailed architecture diagram of the mLab App is provided in [Figure 2](#ocae261-F2). The mLab App was developed using traditional web technologies, including HTML, CSS, and JavaScript, with the Bootstrap framework to build mobile-friendly UI components. The application was designed with a mobile-first approach, ensuring full responsiveness and accessibility across a variety of devices, as mobile devices were anticipated to be the primary means of user access. The described implementation was developed prior to the widespread uptake of more modern web frameworks. Since, mobile and web versions have been built with React and React Native, and have improved the app’s maintainability and extensibility, allowing for easier updates and potential feature expansions.

The backend of the mLab App included a custom API built with PHP that facilitated data handling and communication with the front end. For data storage and secure management, the app utilized REDCap,[18](#ocae261-B18) a research electronic data capture tool, which functioned essentially as a relational database. This choice not only ensured high levels of data security but also enabled easy data access and analysis by nondeveloper members of the research team.

The mLab App was deployed on a dedicated EC2 instance within a virtual private cloud on Amazon Web Services, ensuring a secure and isolated environment for operation. This deployment strategy not only provided robust scalability and reliability but also enhanced data security necessary for handling sensitive health information. Within the same EC2 instance, but accessible only locally and not directly via the web, a custom image-processing library (Python, OpenCV) was hosted to enable analysis of the OraQuick At-Home HIV Test ([Figure 3](#ocae261-F3)). Similar digital analysis work by our group has been described in detail for other infectious disease applications elsewhere[19](#ocae261-B19),[20](#ocae261-B20) and a test-specific algorithm was used to automate the analysis of the OraQuick tests. Participants were provided immediate and personalized linkage to care, if needed. Study team staff received real-time notifications of positive tests (via mLab’s analysis algorithm, or self-reported). Server-side scripts were executed daily to identify users with upcoming test windows and send notifications based on the participant’s notification preferences (eg, Twilio for SMS, SendGrid for email).

### Intervention

The mLab App promotes self-testing and delivers HIV prevention information via email, text, and push notifications and includes an embedded automated mobile phone image-processing algorithm that interprets the results of the OraQuick rapid home HIVST to improve efficiency and accuracy and provides accessible, objective, secure, and real-time feedback on HIV test results. Unlike traditional standalone rapid tests, which can be challenging to interpret, the mLab App automates and simplifies this process by using smartphone imaging and automated image processing for accurate, objective feedback. The mLab App also contains an automated data collection and results reporting feature, which relays test results back to the research team and the study participant, triggering messages that encourage repeat testing, promote PrEP uptake for those who receive a nonreactive test, or facilitate linkage to confirmatory testing and treatment for those with a reactive test. Additionally, it includes a unique data collection and reporting feature for research purposes, ensuring more accurate tracking of testing uptake. The Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research at the FDA reviewed and assigned the mLab App an Investigational Device Exemption for study use with specific requirements including a labeling statement that the mLab App is an investigational device and the participant’s visual interpretation of the OraQuick test is the result of record.

### Randomization

We used block randomization, stratified by site (Chicago, NYC), to assign participants to the study arms in a 2:2:1 ratio of: arm 1—mLab App/Intervention (2), arm 2—Standard of Care/HIV prevention information (2), and arm 3—mailed HIV home tests (1). Treatment assignment was predetermined and blinded, and assignment remained static throughout the course of the trial to reduce allocation bias. Within each city stratum, the REDCap program randomly assigns each participant to the next treatment allocation from a random-permuted block randomized sequence to reduce selection bias.[21](#ocae261-B21)

### Study assessments

The primary outcome is the proportion of participants who self-report an HIV test in the past 6 months. Study visits were conducted at baseline, 6, and 12 months. Beyond self-report, we also collected and analyzed data on the app’s test interpretation performance. Participants in the intervention arm (arm 1) upload a photo image of their OraQuick test, enabling the study team to cross reference self-report with the visual results.

### Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to analyze the baseline characteristics. Intention-to-treat analysis was used with generalized linear mixed logistic models to assess differences in HIV testing rates between the 3 study arms. All models are multivariable with a fixed effect for the study arm, each time point (ie, 6 and 12 months) relative to baseline, and an interaction between the time point and study arm. A random intercept for participants was also added to account for multiple observations of participants. All statistical tests were 2-sided tests with the level of significance at 0.05. We conducted stratified analyses to examine the differences in testing uptake in subgroups (ie, racial/ethnic, age, MSM vs TGW, and risk level). We did not adjust _P_\-values for multiple comparisons as we view this subgroup analysis as exploratory. Effect sizes were compared between arms 1 and 3 to describe differences in testing and linkage behavior attributable to the mLab App with at-home test distribution vs at-home test distribution only. Importantly, mLab was used as the reference group in all models so we can simultaneously evaluate the difference between this group and both other conditions (ie, at-home test and standard care). Therefore, an odds ratio of <1 for an interaction term indicates the superiority of the mLab arm, as this indicates a smaller increase in testing in either condition compared to the mLab arm. To provide greater detail regarding the nature of the model results, we also estimated the specific contrasts for change across each time comparison (ie, Baseline to 6 months, Baseline to 12 months, 6-12 months) between each study arm (ie, At-home testing only vs mLab, Standard care vs mLab, and At-home testing only vs Standard care) which can be found in [Table S1](#sup1). R[22](#ocae261-B22) was used for all statistical analysis, using the glmmTMB[23](#ocae261-B23) package for mixed models and emmeans package for specific contrasts.[24](#ocae261-B24) Missing data were handled via complete case analysis and maximum likelihood estimation.

#### mLab App performance

Participants who used the mLab App submitted photographs of their OraQuick self-test to the app for analysis by an automated image-processing algorithm. Prior to uploading this image for analysis, participants were required to interpret the results of the test using the OraQuick instruction manual. The self-reported result was recorded and uploaded with the user-submitted image of their test. Study team members with expertise in rapid test use and analysis evaluated each uploaded photograph of the self-test and provided an additional assessment. Diagnostic performance metrics (ie, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value) were calculated for the image-processing algorithm of the mLab App using the participant-reported visual result as the gold standard. Additionally, the discordant results between participant visual interpretation and the mLab App image-processing algorithm were compared using McNemar’s test.

Study participants randomized to the mLab App arm completed the Health Information Technology Usability Evaluation Scale (Health-ITUES), a customizable usability assessment instrument at 6 and 12 months. The Health-ITUES has been validated and consists of 20 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (5). A higher scale value indicates higher perceived usability of the technology. The 20-item scale comprised 4 subscales: (1) quality of work life, (2) perceived usefulness, (3) perceived ease of use, and (4) user control.

Overall, study retention rate was 82.9% across all conditions. For mLab App participants, retention was 82.9% at 6 months and 76.3% at 12 months. For standard-of-care participants, retention was 90.8% at both 6 and 12 months. For mailed test arm, retention was 89.5% at 6 months and 84.6% at 12 months. There were no significant associations between participants being lost to follow-up, history of prior HIV testing at baseline, any covariates at baseline, or study arm assignment in a GLMM binomial model.

At baseline, 35.1% of mLab participants reported recent HIV testing, compared to 88.5% at 6 months, and 90.0% at 12 months. For standard-of-care participants, 28.8% reported recent HIV testing at baseline compared to 65.1% at 6 months, and 57.7% at 12 months. For at-home testing-only participants, 21.1% reported recent HIV testing at baseline compared to 72.3% at 6 months and 73.9% at 12 months.

#### mLab App performance

The mLab App analyzed 330 OraQuick At-Home HIV Tests throughout the trial. Only one participant reported a positive visual result, and the confirmatory testing for this participant was HIV-negative. Expert study team member analysis of the participant-uploaded photographs found 100% agreement with the results reported by the participants’ visual interpretation. Using the user-reported visual result as a “gold standard,” the automated image-processing algorithm used by mLab found 273 true negatives, 56 false positives, 1 false negative, and 0 true positives. From these values, the tests uploaded had a 0.3% prevalence, and the image analysis algorithm had an overall accuracy of 82.7%, a true positive rate (sensitivity) of 0%, a true negative rate (specificity) of 83%, a false positive rate of 17%, a false negative rate of 100%, a positive predictive value of 0%, and a negative predictive value of 99.6%. Using McNemar’s Test to compare discordant results between user-reported visual results and the image-processing algorithm, the chi-square statistic was calculated as 53.07 (_P_ < .001).

## Discussion

The mLab App is an mHealth approach to HIV testing through integration of the Oraquick with a smartphone to provide automated, immediate, and confidential reporting and to help support linkage to care for either HIV care or PrEP services. The app provides a comprehensive HIV prevention tool (reminders for testing, recommendations for follow-up), automated reporting, error-checking, and linkage to care, and addressing barriers to HIV self-testing. Our study findings provide evidence of the efficacy of the mLab App among young MSM in the United States and demonstrate that technology augments and supports testing and interpretation of results. It has the potential to address many of the current barriers to self-testing kits. The mLab App user interface promotes a holistic diagnostic experience because it provides step-by-step error-checking with clear picture instructions.

The mLab App demonstrated evidence of efficacy, with mLab App arm participants demonstrating an increase from 35.1% reporting HIV testing in the prior 6 months compared to 88.5% at 6 months. In contrast, 28.8% of control participants reported an HIV test at baseline, which only increased to 65.1% at 6 months. In a generalized linear mixed model estimating this change and controlling for multiple observations of participants, this equated to control participants reporting a 71.4% smaller increase in HIV testing relative to mLab participants (_P_ = .001) at 6 months. In addition, this difference was maintained at 12 months with control participants reporting an 82.9% smaller increase relative to mLab App participants (_P_ < .001) from baseline to 12 months.

In addition to efficacy to increase HIV self-testing, the mLab App offers practical advantages to the individual who completes the test, the testing team/provider, and also has value in a research context. For the individual who completes the test, mLab provides testing reminders, interpretation of the test result, history of test results, as well as HIV prevention education. From a provider perspective, the mLab App provides a hub for receiving, interpreting, and exchanging information about the HIVST with the person tested, to support the testing process. Because the individual who completes the test uploads both an image of the test and their interpretation of the test result, the testing team has an opportunity to validate their interpretation and take immediate action if the test is interpreted incorrectly. In addition, the provider receives a real-time automated message if the image algorithm suggests a reactive test, which prompts them to contact the individual and link them to HIV confirmatory testing. Finally, the mLab App offers advantages to HIVST researchers because it provides an objective measure of an HIVST result, the validation of which is automated.

The CDC High Impact HIV/AIDS Prevention Project publishes and continually updates a Compendium of HIV Prevention Interventions with Evidence of Effectiveness.[25](#ocae261-B25) Currently, this compendium includes 15 HIV Testing Interventions. Of these interventions, several are structural interventions with 3 focused on electronic health record alerts,[26–28](#ocae261-B26) 2 are clinic-based interventions,[29](#ocae261-B29),[30](#ocae261-B30) 1 is a telephone-based counseling intervention,[31](#ocae261-B31) and 1 is a partner testing intervention.[32](#ocae261-B32) The largest number of interventions (_n_ = 7) focus on the provision of free HIVST[33–39](#ocae261-B33) which is comparable to arm 2 of this study. Only 1 study, M-cubed, leverages mobile technology for promoting HIV testing.[40](#ocae261-B40) Nonetheless, the mLab App, described in this study is quite different than the M-cubed app which provides sexual health messaging and is not a holistic tool integrated with the HIVST.[41](#ocae261-B41) In summary, the mLab App provides a unique contribution to current HIV prevention intervention tools and offers innovation via a mobile platform for reaching people who may not be willing or able to access clinic-based services.

In support of the FDA Investigational Device Exemption application for this study, an extensive laboratory analysis was performed prior to the enrollment of participants to determine the optimal image-processing parameters of the mLab App (eg, thresholding, filtering, registration), ideal conditions for capturing a photograph of the HIVST (eg, background, lighting, HIVST orientation, camera-to-test distance), and performance in nonideal conditions (eg, nonuniform lighting, photographs taken at nonoptimal angles of incidence and distances). During this laboratory testing of the mLab App, the true negative rate was 75.7% and 85% in nonideal and ideal benchtop conditions, respectively; the true positive rate was 98.5% and 99.3% in nonideal and ideal benchtop conditions, respectively. From the self-tests evaluated during the trial, the true negative rate (specificity) of the image-processing algorithm was 83.0%, comparable to that found in ideal conditions during laboratory testing. With no true positive tests throughout the study, a comparison from the trial to that of laboratory testing for the true positive rate (sensitivity) is not particularly meaningful.

Laboratory measurements of sensitivity and specificity, and the specificity during the trial of the mLab App, exceed suggested benchmarks for net clinical benefit.[42](#ocae261-B42) Specificity of the mLab App during the trial was comparable, albeit slightly lower, to other studies that used the OraQuick At-Home HIV Test.[43](#ocae261-B43),[44](#ocae261-B44) Diagnostic performance is typically a tradeoff between sensitivity and specificity. Selection of algorithm parameters can aim for a balance of the 2, or, depending on the use case, favor one outcome over another. As the OraQuick Self-Test is a screening tool, and the mLab App was used as part of a research study, the parameters of the mLab App’s image-processing algorithm were biased to reduce the number of false negatives. The significant result from McNemar’s test indicates that there indeed was a significant difference in how the image-processing algorithm performed relative to visual inspection in the instances where the 2 measurements disagreed. Parameters that balanced sensitivity and specificity were identified during laboratory testing of the mLab App, and their usage in this study, given its low prevalence, may have improved statistical performance through fewer false positives. Since the initial FDA IDE application in 2018, when the mLab App’s image-processing algorithm was approved and parameters were fixed, we[20](#ocae261-B20) and others.[45–48](#ocae261-B45) have demonstrated even further technological improvements in sensitivity, specificity, photo quality control, and other technological advancements using digital image analysis for mobile-phone based interpretation of rapid diagnostic tests.

Despite TGW having the highest burden of HIV and carrying the greatest lifetime risk for acquisition of HIV among all racial, ethnic, and sexual gender minority groups, our study struggled to recruit and enroll TGW with only 12 TGW enrolled. In a recent CDC Surveillance study, 42% of TGW received a positive HIV test result, pointing to the high prevalence of HIV among TGW.[49](#ocae261-B49),[50](#ocae261-B50) Nonetheless, recruitment of TGW into research studies can be challenging given that many of these women are subject to stigma and have detrimental socioeconomic factors, which make research study engagement and retention difficult.[51–53](#ocae261-B51) Given that our study included both MSM and TGW and we did not have quota sampling, our findings specific to TGW are limited.

The mLab App demonstrates the potential of mobile health applications to improve accessibility and privacy for underserved or hard-to-reach patient populations while enabling accurate and timely healthcare delivery. Following its successful deployment and efficacy evaluation, integrating the app with existing healthcare infrastructure is a logical next step. The version used in this study was built to meet all compliance and security requirements necessary for such integration, ensuring patient confidentiality. During the research study, mLab results were stored as JSON files and securely transmitted via the REDCap API. For broader clinical use, these data payloads could be easily refactored into the appropriate HL7 FHIR resources for seamless exchange with electronic health records (EHRs). Once transformed, the results could be written directly to EHRs, rather than research databases, through the existing APIs of individual EHR systems or via third-party middleware solutions (eg, Redox, HumanAPI, 1upHealth, Xealth). Such integration with health records would also allow clinicians and care staff to receive notifications on patient HIV self-testing, from the EHR software they are currently using rather than a new app. This approach has already proven successful in transferring patient-reported outcomes from REDCap to EHRs[54](#ocae261-B54) in the rapid integration of digital health apps into EHRs during the COVID-19 pandemic,[55](#ocae261-B55) and with the growing adoption of wearable devices integrated into health records.[56](#ocae261-B56)

### Limitations

This study was limited to young MSM and TGW living in the New York City and Chicago metropolitan areas. Future studies should consider assessing the effect of this intervention in broader geographic areas with special consideration given to HIV prevention deserts in the United States where resources and access to these services are scant. Notably, none of the study participants tested HIV+ during the study period. There may be several reasons, and each is important to consider when contextualizing the findings of this study. Overall HIV incidence in the United States remains low even in high-risk populations especially in comparison to Sub-Saharan Africa where the epidemic continues to plague the continent. There may be a sampling bias in that the participants who enrolled in this study are more engaged in the preservation of their health and therefore may be at lower risk than other men with similar risk profiles. Finally, participants who tested positive in the standard of care or mailed home kit arms may not have notified our study team and may decide not to continue with our research study.

## Conclusions

Using the CDC compendium criteria[25](#ocae261-B25) as a framework, these findings suggest that the mLab App is a well-supported, evidence-based, behavioral risk-reduction intervention for increasing HIV testing among young MSM. Importantly this app had broader implications for the informatics community and for integration with EHRs. The app has the potential for allowing patients to interface with their providers from home through self-testing and seeking follow-up care remotely.
