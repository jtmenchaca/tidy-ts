## Abstract

### Background

Mild cognitive impairment and early-stage dementia significantly impact healthcare utilization and costs, yet more than half of affected patients remain underdiagnosed. This study leverages audio-recorded patient-nurse verbal communication in home healthcare settings to develop an artificial intelligence-based screening tool for early detection of cognitive decline.

### Objective

To develop a speech processing algorithm using routine patient-nurse verbal communication and evaluate its performance when combined with electronic health record (EHR) data in detecting early signs of cognitive decline.

### Method

We analyzed 125 audio-recorded patient-nurse verbal communication for 47 patients from a major home healthcare agency in New York City. Out of 47 patients, 19 experienced symptoms associated with the onset of cognitive decline. A natural language processing algorithm was developed to extract domain-specific linguistic and interaction features from these recordings. The algorithm’s performance was compared against EHR-based screening methods. Both standalone and combined data approaches were assessed using F1-score and area under the curve (AUC) metrics.

### Results

The initial model using only patient-nurse verbal communication achieved an F1-score of 85 and an AUC of 86.47. The model based on EHR data achieved an F1-score of 75.56 and an AUC of 79. Combining patient-nurse verbal communication with EHR data yielded the highest performance, with an F1-score of 88.89 and an AUC of 90.23. Key linguistic indicators of cognitive decline included reduced linguistic diversity, grammatical challenges, repetition, and altered speech patterns. Incorporating audio data significantly enhanced the risk prediction models for hospitalization and emergency department visits.

### Discussion

Routine verbal communication between patients and nurses contains critical linguistic and interactional indicators for identifying cognitive impairment. Integrating audio-recorded patient-nurse communication with EHR data provides a more comprehensive and accurate method for early detection of cognitive decline, potentially improving patient outcomes through timely interventions. This combined approach could revolutionize cognitive impairment screening in home healthcare settings.

**Keywords:** cognitive impairment, home healthcare, patient-nurse verbal communication, screening algorithms, machine learning, natural language processing

## Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias (ADRD) pose significant challenges to public health,[1](#ocae300-B1) with projections suggesting up to 11-16 million affected individuals by 2050.[2–4](#ocae300-B2) Despite extensive healthcare utilization and associated costs, over 50% of these cases remain undiagnosed, often due to limited biomarker availability and the subtleties of early symptom recognition.[5–7](#ocae300-B5) Recognizing the urgency for improved detection methods, the National Institute on Aging has prioritized the development of artificial intelligence (AI)-based screening tools for early detection of patients at risk of cognitive decline.

Conventional screening algorithms that rely on electronic health record (EHR) data have shown moderate success in detecting cognitive decline, largely due to incomplete documentation of subtle early cognitive cues. In a previous study, we used natural language processing (NLP) techniques, including topic modeling and a semisupervised approach with Word2Vec embeddings, to extract clinical, behavioral, and social risk factors from nursing notes in home healthcare. By training machine learning (ML) algorithms—support vector machines, ensemble decision trees, and logistic regression—on the NLP-derived factors and structured EHR data, we achieved a sensitivity of 0.75, an F1-score of 0.62, and an AUC of 0.76.[8](#ocae300-B8),[9](#ocae300-B9)

The performance of screening algorithms in outpatient settings has varied, often demonstrating only moderate effectiveness. Nori et al,[10](#ocae300-B10) for instance, reported a sensitivity of 0.47 and an AUC of 0.87 for predicting ADRD, using administrative claims and EHR data from OptumLabs. In another study, Mao et al employed the Bio+Clinical BERT model, a version of Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) pretrained on contextualized embeddings. After fine-tuning the model on clinical notes from patients with a history of mild cognitive impairment, they achieved an AUC of 0.88 and an F1-score of 0.68 for detecting ADRD, utilizing a fully connected network layer to integrate the features extracted by Bio+Clinical BERT model.[11](#ocae300-B11)

Emerging studies indicate that language impairments, such as speech inaccuracies,[12](#ocae300-B12) word-finding difficulty,[13](#ocae300-B13),[14](#ocae300-B14) and disfluencies,[13](#ocae300-B13),[15](#ocae300-B15) are strong predictors of cognitive decline, suggesting speech could serve as a biomarker for early detection. These linguistic cues can be identified using NLP methods. However, most studies rely on lab-recorded speech, where participants perform brief tasks like reading sentences. While useful for detecting impairments such as Alzheimer’s disease, this approach often misses early stages of cognitive decline due to the lack of critical linguistic and social cues, such as pauses and communication patterns. Our previous research demonstrated the feasibility of recording routine patient-clinician interactions in home healthcare as an important data source for assessing health status. These recordings capture subtle linguistic and interaction cues,[16](#ocae300-B16),[17](#ocae300-B17) providing sensitive indicators for the early detection of cognitive decline.

In home healthcare settings, which predominantly serve individuals aged 65 years and older with chronic health conditions,[5](#ocae300-B5) a significant number of patients experiencing cognitive decline remain undiagnosed; our recent study found that 63% were not formally identified.[18](#ocae300-B18),[19](#ocae300-B19) Factors contributing to this diagnostic gap include inadequate communication of cognitive status during patient handoffs, the emergence of new cognitive issues after hospitalization, and the absence of thorough cognitive assessments.[20](#ocae300-B20) Comprehensive evaluations are time-consuming and require detailed interviews with patients and caregivers and specialized training for administering and interpreting tests. These challenges hinder home healthcare nurses—who are uniquely positioned to detect cognitive decline—from developing and implementing targeted care plans. Consequently, delays and inaccuracies in diagnosis increase the risk of adverse outcomes like falls and emergency department visits.

This study aims to develop and validate a speech processing pipeline using NLP and ML techniques to detect cognitive decline in home healthcare patients through patient-nurse verbal communication. Additionally, it seeks to evaluate the added value of incorporating these processed verbal communications into an EHR-based screening algorithm that includes clinical notes. We hypothesize that integrating patient-nurse verbal communications with EHR data can significantly improve the algorithm’s sensitivity in the early detection of cognitive decline.

### Study setting, participant eligibility, and audio-recording patient-nurse verbal communication

This research was conducted at VNS Health, a large, diverse, not-for-profit home healthcare agency in the United States, and received institutional review board approval (IRB #E20-003). We recruited 5 registered nurses who provided home care to older adults and agreed to audio-record their patient encounters. Nurses introduced the study to patients, and a research assistant approached interested participants to obtain informed consent.

#### Eligibility criteria

Patients over 60, proficient in English, without a diagnosis for severe cognitive impairments or major neurological disorders like Alzheimer’s disease, and without significant conditions like chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) or heart failure were eligible for participation in this study. Participants were excluded if they had any speech impairment affecting communication with the nurse, required caregiver assistance for communication, or were unable to independently comprehend and sign the informed consent form.

#### Audio-recording

We evaluated several devices for recording patient-nurse verbal communication and chose the Saramonic Blink658 (see [Appendix S1](#sup1)) for its usability and audio quality.[21](#ocae300-B21) This portable device, with dual wireless microphones that attach to clothing, provides clear speech transmission to devices like an iPod and offers dual-channel storage. Both patients and nurses found the recording process acceptable, with no interference in communication. The audio was transcribed using Amazon Web Services General Transcribe (AWS-GT) for its low word error rate of 26%, which outperforms alternatives such as AWS Medical Transcribe and Wave2Vec.

### Datasets: patient-nurse verbal communication and EHR data

#### Sample of audio-recorded patient-nurse verbal communication

We audio-recorded 125 encounters involving 47 patients and transcribed them using AWS-GT. Among these patients, 18 (38%) had 1 recording, 15 (32%) had 2, and 14 (30%) had 3 or more. The 125 encounters included 8099 utterances, with patients contributing 4016 and nurses 4083. Additionally, we collected EHR data for the study sample, including: (1) Structured Clinical Data—covering demographics, medical history, and daily functional status; (2) Free-Text Clinical Notes—nurses’ entries providing narrative evaluations of the patient’s conditions and documenting related communication with the care team and administrative tasks.

#### Outcome of interest

To ensure the study focused on early detection of cognitive decline, patients were selected using EHR data, excluding those with ADRD based on International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes. Further exclusions were applied to eliminate confounding conditions such as Parkinson’s disease, brain tumors, and hearing difficulties, ensuring the analysis focused on speech-related indicators of cognitive decline. The research assistants screened eligible patients, verifying English proficiency, assessing comprehension during the consent process, and ensuring patients could communicate independently with nurses. Following consent, skilled home healthcare nurses performed detailed assessments to identify early signs of cognitive decline. These assessments included interviews with patients and caregivers to evaluate symptoms including “confusion,” “decline in mental, emotional, or behavioral status in the past 3 months,” and “decline in current (day of assessment) level of alertness, orientation, comprehension, concentration, and immediate memory for simple commands.” Additionally, patients were evaluated for “disruptive symptoms,” “impaired decision-making,” and “physical aggression.” A neurology expert on our team confirmed that these indicators are commonly associated with early stages of cognitive decline. Among the 47 patients assessed using these additional methods, 19 exhibited symptoms consistent with early stages of cognitive decline. See [Appendix S2](#sup1) for more details.

### Analyzing patient-nurse verbal communication

Analyzing patient-nurse verbal communication involves 3 components. (1) We analyzed the patient’s speech to assess the patient’s ability in syntactic and semantic aspects of language organization. (2) We examined the interactions between patients and nurses to extract interaction cues that may indicate cognitive decline. (3) We analyze the nurses’ language during these communications to understand how it may influence or reflect the patient’s cognitive state.

#### Component 1. Analyzing the patient’s speech to assess the patient’s ability in syntactic and semantic aspects of language organization

1.  Measuring lexical diversity, syntactic complexity, and semantic coherence: Early cognitive decline commonly involves memory and semantic fluency impairments, observable through symptoms and formal testing. Subtle early language changes often progress to pronounced logopenic aphasia in later stages. Individuals with speech impairment due to cognitive decline often exhibit less structured speech planning[22](#ocae300-B22) difficulties in word retrieval,[13](#ocae300-B13) simplified syntax and semantics,[13](#ocae300-B13),[23](#ocae300-B23) and frequently rely on circumlocution.[13](#ocae300-B13),[15](#ocae300-B15) These symptoms can lead to communication errors and diminished verbal clarity. Although these linguistic cues are challenging to detect via direct observation or traditional neuropsychological assessments, they are identifiable through advanced speech processing algorithms that leverage NLP techniques. To systematically analyze these cues, we employ domain-specific language features informed by established research on lexical diversity, syntactic complexity, and semantic coherence. Detailed measurements for each feature are presented in [Table 1](#ocae300-T1).
    
2.  Identifying psycholinguistic cues using Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count: Previous studies have shown that certain semantic cues may indicate cognitive decline.[36](#ocae300-B36),[37](#ocae300-B37) To analyze these cues, we used the Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC) 2015,[38](#ocae300-B38) a lexical-based NLP tool developed by language psychology experts. It contains a large selection of commonly used words organized into 10 main categories, including linguistic, affective, social, and cognitive processes. In healthcare, many studies have confirmed LIWC’s reliability and validity in identifying psycholinguistic cues related to mental and neurological disorders,[23](#ocae300-B23),[39–42](#ocae300-B39) particularly cognitive decline.[23](#ocae300-B23)
    
3.  Encoding semantic and syntactic errors using transformer models: In addition to domain-specific language features, we utilized pretrained transformer-based models to encode linguistic cues associated with cognitive decline. Transformer models like BERT and its derivatives (eg, DistilBERT, BioClinicalBERT) excel at capturing long-term dependencies text, producing contextualized vectors that represent word meanings and interrelations. Leveraging the attention mechanism, these models effectively distinguish between fluent sentences and those containing semantic or syntactic errors.

Our previous research indicates that combining transformer models with domain-specific language features enhances the accuracy of cognitive impairment detection algorithms. Notably, our recent study showed that using DistilBERT on a small speech dataset of patients with cognitive decline outperformed other transformer models in detecting relevant cues. DistilBERT retains 97% of BERT’s performance while being 40% smaller and 60% faster, making it ideal when balancing computational efficiency and performance. We used the cased version of this model with the following hyperparameters: 6 layers, 768 hidden units, 12 _attention_ heads, and 65 M parameters.[43](#ocae300-B43) The model implementation is available here.[43](#ocae300-B43)

#### Component 2. Analyzing communication between patients and nurses to extract interaction cues

Patients with cognitive decline often exhibit deficits in recognizing interaction cues, which impair their ability to engage in verbal communication.[44](#ocae300-B44),[45](#ocae300-B45) In home healthcare settings, analyzing patient-nurse interactions can reveal these subtle communication impairments. To investigate this, we first used a classifier developed in our previous study[46](#ocae300-B46),[47](#ocae300-B47) to identify the speaker labels (patient or nurse) in the audio-recordings, leveraging AWS Transcribe’s speaker diarization information. Next, using the speaker labels and word timestamps provided by AWS Transcribe, along with the Natural Language Toolkit (NLTK) in Python, we quantified parameters of social interaction,[48](#ocae300-B48),[49](#ocae300-B49) including the total number of patient turns, dialogue interactivity, turn density, turn duration, and the relative timing of turns.

#### Component 3. Analyzing nurses’ language during patient-nurse communication

Building upon our previous research,[50](#ocae300-B50) we observed that the dynamics of verbal communication between patients and nurses are influenced by the patient’s health condition, particularly among those at risk of adverse outcomes. Nurses often modify their sentence structure and vocabulary when communicating with patients experiencing cognitive decline, simplifying their language to accommodate the patients’ comprehension challenges. To analyze the language used by nurses, we employed 2 methods:

1.  We utilized BioClinicalBERT, a transformer-based, domain-specific model trained on an extensive corpus of medical literature and clinical notes from sources such as PubMed, PubMed Central, and Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III (MIMIC III). BioClinicalBERT outperforms other BERT derivatives in health-related NLP tasks, especially those involving medical professional language, making it highly effective for encoding the nuanced language used in clinical settings. We used the cased version of BioClinicalBERT with the following hyperparameters: 12 layers, 768 hidden units, 12 attention heads, and approximately 110 million parameters.[51](#ocae300-B51)
    
2.  LIWC analysis: Given the impact of patient health status on communication dynamics, we employed the LIWC tool to extract linguistic features from nurses’ language. Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count allows for the quantification of various linguistic dimensions, providing insights into the psychological and social aspects of communication.
    

We hypothesize that integrating the linguistic cues from nurses speech with patient speech can improve the performance of screening algorithm for detecting cognitive decline.

### Analyzing clinical notes in the EHR system

Clinical notes are integral components of EHRs, providing comprehensive narratives of patient health conditions. To analyze these notes effectively, we employed a 2-method approach:

1.  BioClinicalBERT encoding: We used BioClinicalBERT, a domain-specific language model, to capture the nuanced and contextually rich language present in clinical notes. This model facilitates a deep linguistic analysis by encoding complex linguistic patterns and subtle semantic information inherent in the clinical narratives.
    
2.  Unified Medical Language System (UMLS) integration: To extract and standardize references to medical concepts and activities (eg, sleep disorder, anxiety), we leveraged the UMLS. Unified Medical Language System integrates over 100 biomedical terminologies, mapping more than 1.5 million medical concepts to unique identifiers (CUIs) with specific names and semantic types—for instance, “sleep disorder” is classified as a “Sign/Symptom.” We employed QuickUMLS to match terms in the clinical notes to their corresponding CUIs and semantic types, ensuring precise extraction and classification of medical information.
    

### Building and evaluating ML classifiers

1.  Feature selection: To identify the most relevant features from patient-nurse verbal communication (components 1, 2, and 3) and EHR data for building the screening algorithms, we employed Joint Mutual Information Maximization (JMIM).[52](#ocae300-B52),[53](#ocae300-B53) Joint Mutual Information Maximization optimizes feature selection by maximizing mutual information with the target outcome while minimizing redundancy between features. This method is particularly effective for generalizing in scenarios with small sample sizes and large feature sets.[52](#ocae300-B52),[53](#ocae300-B53)
    
2.  ML classifiers: We used logistic regression as the baseline model for its efficiency and transparency in binary classification, providing a clear benchmark against more complex algorithms. Extreme Gradient Boosting[54](#ocae300-B54) (XGBoost) was selected due to its advanced handling of varied data structures and its ability to boost weak learners through a highly efficient gradient boosting framework, making it particularly effective for the study’s diverse dataset features. Support vector machine[55](#ocae300-B55) (SVM): Employed with both radial basis function (RBF) and Linear kernels, SVMs are powerful classifiers capable of handling high-dimensional data. The Linear kernel is suitable for linearly separable data, while the RBF kernel enables modeling of nonlinear relationships, enhancing the classifier’s flexibility and performance.
    
3.  Screening algorithms: The screening algorithms were built iteratively on:
    
    1.  Patient speech: We constructed ML classifiers based on key linguistic and interaction cues identified through JMIM, as outlined in components 1 and 2. These features were then fused with contextual word embedding features extracted from DistilBERT (component 1). Due to variability in the number of audio-recorded communications per patient (ranging from 1 to over 3), we aggregated these into a single document per patient. Our previous research showed that incorporating all communication data significantly enhances the algorithms’ ability to identify patients at risk of adverse outcomes.[50](#ocae300-B50)
        
    2.  Integrating patient speech and nurse speech: Next, we built ML classifiers on important features extracted from patient speech and nurses speech specified in “component 3: Analyzing the nurses’ language.”
        
    3.  EHR data, including clinical notes: We built the classifier using feature sets derived from clinical notes, structured demographic information and patient medical history available in the home healthcare EHR data. Similar to patient-nurse communication, the number of clinical notes per patient varied based on the number of home healthcare visits. Consequently, we consolidated all available clinical notes for each patient into a single document for classifier construction. The most informative features extracted from clinical notes were integrated with EHR data to build the classifier.
        
    4.  Integrating patient-nurse communications with EHR data (A + B + C): We combined the informative features of patient-nurse verbal communication with the informative features of EHR data through late fusion. Our prior research demonstrated that a late fusion model outperforms other fusion methods, especially in the context of small datasets.[56](#ocae300-B56) We hypothesize that this integration of verbal communication and EHR data can enhance the classifier’s ability to identify patients with cognitive decline.
        
4.  Performance evaluation of ML classifiers: In evaluating the performance of our ML classifiers, we employed leave-one-out cross-validation along with standard performance metrics, including area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC), F1-score, and F2-score. area under the receiver operating characteristic curve is the tradeoff between the true positive rate (sensitivity) and the false positive rate (1-specificity) and has the advantage of being invariant to the class distribution. The F1-score, representing the harmonic mean of sensitivity and precision, is particularly valuable in this study given the imbalanced dataset, as it provides a balanced assessment of the classifier’s precision and recall. Additionally, we employed the F2-score, which places greater emphasis on sensitivity, making it especially suitable for this study, where the accurate identification of positive cases of cognitive decline is crucial. See [Appendix S3](#sup1) for more details. Accuracy was excluded as a metric due to its potential to provide misleading results in the context of imbalanced data.

### Performance of BERT models

When we evaluated the performance of DistilBERT using only patient verbal communication to assess the model’s ability to detect linguistic cues of cognitive decline, we achieved an F1-score of 69.39 (AUC = 69.36). This performance was higher than that of BioClinicalBERT (F1-score = 61.54, AUC-ROC = 64.29) and the BERT base model (F1-score = 65, AUC-ROC = 66.73). These findings align with our previous study, which indicated that DistilBERT had superior performance in encoding linguistic cues associated with cognitive impairment, particularly in small sample size datasets. Conversely, when we measured the performance of the BERT models on clinical notes, BioClinicalBERT achieved an F1-score of 64.29 and an AUC-ROC of 69.17, outperforming BERT (F1-score = 65.52, AUC-ROC = 67.11) and DistilBERT (F1-score = 64.15, AUC-ROC = 66.35). This result is consistent with our previous findings indicating that BioClinicalBERT, when applied to clinicians’ professional language, can provide better results compared to other BERT models for encoding the linguistic cues associated with cognitive decline.

### Important features in patient-nurse verbal communication associated with cognitive decline

1.  Important linguistic features in the patient’s speech associated with cognitive decline: [Figure 3A](#ocae300-F3), based on JMIM analysis, illustrates key linguistic features associated with cognitive decline, including lexical diversity, syntactic complexity, and semantic coherence. Lower Root and Corrected Type-Token Ratios suggest reduced linguistic diversity in affected patients. The increase in lexical frequency and a lower unique-to-total word count ratio suggests a more limited vocabulary. Changes in the usage patterns of articles, verbs, and nouns may reflect grammatical difficulties linked to cognitive decline. Furthermore, increased SD in clause similarity and higher proportions of clauses with zero similarity, along with altered speech rate, highlight the impact on semantic networks and speech fluidity characteristic of language impairment in patients experiencing cognitive decline.
    
2.  Important interaction cues associated with cognitive decline: [Figure 3B](#ocae300-F3), based on the JMIM analysis of communication patterns, indicates that longer encounters are characterized by a higher SD in speaking times for both patients and nurses, along with an increased coefficient of variation in patient speaking time. Moreover, patients with cognitive impairment tend to have longer periods of silence, and nurses in these encounters exhibit increased total speaking time.
    
3.  Important psycholinguistic cues associated with cognitive decline: [Figure 3C and D](#ocae300-F3), as analyzed in the JMIM study of LIWC, features for patient and nurse language. Additionally, patients referenced future-related terms, such as “Future,” as well as personal and social topics like “Friends” and “Health.” These patterns may indicate a focus on upcoming plans, social interactions, and health concerns. Increased references to “Cognitive Processes,” “Work,” and “Differentiation” were also observed, which could suggest shifts in mental processing or professional engagement.

## Discussion

This study is the first to show that routine verbal communication between patients and nurses in home healthcare settings contains critical linguistic and interactional indicators for identifying cognitive impairment. Analyzing patient verbal communication alone yielded an F1-score of 0.75. However, incorporating nurse communication improved performance by 13.3%, reaching an F1-score of 0.85. This increase highlights a significant enhancement in predictive accuracy and underscores the impact of patient cognitive status on nurse communicative behavior. Conversely, relying solely on EHR data, including clinical notes, the F1-score was 75.56, demonstrating fewer cues in EHR data than patient-nurse interactions. Yet, integrating verbal communication with EHR data achieved the highest F1-score, 88.89, indicating that the combined approach provides a more comprehensive and accurate method for detecting cognitive impairment.

The most significant indicators of cognitive decline in verbal communication include diminished linguistic diversity, grammatical challenges in sentence construction, repetition at the level of words and clauses, reduced word similarity, slower speech rate, and variability and irregularity in speech patterns. To extract these domain-specific features, we built an NLP algorithm using popular toolkits such as NLTK and interaction-related features, including turn-taking information extracted using AWS General Transcribe. Compared to word embedding features obtained from pretrained transformer models like DistilBERT and BioClinicalBERT, these feature sets provide direct insight into specific language and interaction problems of patients. However, as our previous study showed,[50](#ocae300-B50),[56](#ocae300-B56) transformer models can encode nuances in semantic and syntactic patterns of patient language, adding value to the screening algorithm for cognitive impairment detection when incorporated with domain-specific features.

In addition to these NLP features, we utilized the LIWC, a tool designed to analyze the psychological aspects of language. Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count has demonstrated utility in numerous studies for assessing patient health status and predicting adverse outcomes such as depression and emergency department visits. Our analysis revealed that patients with cognitive decline often exhibit language fluency issues, including increased use of filler words and heightened concerns about health, social interactions, and work. When examining nurse communication with these patients, key features included an emphasis on family and financial aspects, reflecting the patients’ needs for social and financial support. Furthermore, the tone adopted by nurses is crucial, potentially indicating empathy in response to the patients’ communication challenges. Nurses’ use of negation terms often involves correcting or redirecting patients’ statements, which may frequently occur in conversations with individuals showing early signs of cognitive impairment. This pattern may result from patients’difficulties in recalling specific information or constructing accurate statements, prompting more frequent corrective interjections by nurses.

Furthermore, our findings highlight specific communication dynamics indicative of cognitive impairment. The variability and irregularity in speech patterns, highlighted by the high SD and coefficient of variation in speaking times, may indicate difficulties in maintaining consistent communication flows. The extended periods of silence observed in patients could reflect struggles in retrieving or processing information. Additionally, the increased speaking time of nurses might represent their efforts to compensate for these communication barriers, necessitating additional explanations, clarifying questions, and enhanced efforts to ensure patient understanding.

Detecting cognitive impairment in its earliest stages is critical for provides opportunity timely interventions that can slow cognitive decline and improve patients’ quality of life.[57](#ocae300-B57) Early identification helps plan appropriate care, implement safety measures, and manage comorbid conditions more effectively. Therapeutic interventions are increasingly recognized as being time-dependent. Moreover, they are also expanding from symptom-based therapies like cholinesterase inhibitors to biological based treatments such as recently FDA-approved monoclonal antibody therapies.[58](#ocae300-B58) Lifestyle modifications, including cognitive training, physical exercise, and dietary changes, may play a role in slowing the progression of cognitive decline and preventing early complications, such as progressive immobility. These interventions can enhance patient engagement in their healthcare by promoting participation in activities that optimized chances of maintaining cognitive and physical function and delaying or avoiding their complications. From a research perspective, early detection provides valuable data for studying the cognitive decline and evaluating interventions’ effectiveness.[57](#ocae300-B57) Integrating audio-recorded patient-nurse verbal communication into home healthcare workflows and EHR data offers a comprehensive and accurate method for identifying cognitive impairment, leading to better care and outcomes.

Analysis of patient-clinician verbal communication has been a focal point in various studies.[59–61](#ocae300-B59) For instance, Drew et al[59](#ocae300-B59) analyzed patient-practitioner communication to identify practitioners’ communication patterns in addressing patient concerns. In another study, Mejdahl et al[61](#ocae300-B61) analyzed patient-clinician communication in epilepsy outpatient clinics to explore the impact of patients’ self-reported data on the epilepsy treatment outcome. We also showed that incorporating patient-nurse verbal communication with EHR data can significantly improve the risk of hospitalization and emergency department visits in home healthcare.[50](#ocae300-B50) Despite these potential benefits, audio-recording patient-clinician communication is not standard practice, primarily due to clinicians’ reluctance to record their conversations with patients, logistical complexities, and the absence of clear policies and guidelines from administration and management.[21](#ocae300-B21) As a result, there is a significant gap in the availability of audio-recorded data necessary for developing robust speech processing pipelines aimed at analyzing patient-clinician communication patterns, understanding their concerns, and developing sensitive screening algorithms for mental and cognitive impairment detection.[21](#ocae300-B21)

Conducting feasibility studies is essential for understanding the practical aspects of integrating audio-recording technology into clinical workflows and identifying facilitators and barriers. These studies will address privacy concerns, technological requirements, and the impact on patient and clinician communication patterns, paving the way for effective implementation and enhanced patient care. In our previous studies in home healthcare settings, we conducted pilot studies to identify effective audio-recording patient-nurse verbal communication procedures.[21](#ocae300-B21) The findings of these studies showed that both patients and nurses were comfortable with the audio-recording procedure, and patients found it particularly useful for personal use (eg, reviewing the clinician’s instruction).[21](#ocae300-B21) The involvement of healthcare stakeholders, especially home healthcare clinicians and managers, plays a crucial role in integrating audio-recording into home healthcare workflows and establishing the necessary processing methods for continued use in patient care management.[62](#ocae300-B62),[63](#ocae300-B63) With recent advancements in AI technology, home healthcare clinical workflows could soon include risk prediction models based on these audio-recordings, enhancing the speed of identifying risks. These models could serve as the foundation for clinical decision support systems, enabling clinicians to promptly identify patients whose health is declining and intervene to avert negative outcomes.

The study has several limitations. First, we relied on a relatively modest sample size of 125 audio-recorded patient-nurse conversations from 47 patients, all collected from one of the largest home healthcare agencies in New York City. The generalizability of its primary findings outside of this setting is uncertain. Second, while using pretrained transformer models, DistilBERT and BioClinicalBERT, for analyzing patient verbal communication and clinical notes and employing a leave-one-out validation technique offered an unbiased view of our model, the single-source nature of our data could potentially limit the further generalizability of our findings. Third, the study’s focus on textual data neglects acoustic features such as intonation and speech rate, which are pivotal for comprehensive cognitive impairment analysis. We did not explore the effectiveness of other feature-generation methods and ML classifiers for building screening algorithms. Given the natural setup of recording patient-nurse interactions in our study, it is unclear whether this influenced our primary findings. However, feedback from both nurses and patients indicated that the recording process did not noticeably affect the delivery of care or alter behavior compared to unrecorded evaluations.

In the future, it will be beneficial to expand the research to involve data from multiple sites, investigate the effectiveness of acoustic features such as speech fluency, intensity, and rhythmic structure of verbal communication, and utilize deep learning methods and fusion frameworks to effectively combine feature sets from multiple data sources.

## Conclusions

This study is the first to demonstrate that routine verbal communication between patients and nurses in home healthcare contains important linguistic and interactional cues for identifying cognitive impairment. By integrating these cues with EHR data, we significantly improved the screening algorithm’s predictive performance. To implement this approach effectively, feasibility studies should be conducted to address privacy concerns, technological requirements, and the impact on communication patterns. These studies have the potential to pave the way for integrating audio-recorded patient-nurse interactions into clinical workflows, creating new opportunities for early detection of cognitive impairment and improving patient care and outcomes.
