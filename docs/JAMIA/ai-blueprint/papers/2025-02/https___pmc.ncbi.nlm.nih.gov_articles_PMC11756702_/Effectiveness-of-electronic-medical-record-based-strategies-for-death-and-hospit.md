## Abstract

### Objective

Event capture in clinical trials is resource-intensive, and electronic medical records (EMRs) offer a potential solution. This study develops algorithms for EMR-based death and hospitalization capture and compares them with traditional event capture methods.

### Materials and Methods

We compared the effectiveness of EMR-based event capture and site-captured events adjudicated by a clinical endpoint committee in the multi-center INfluenza Vaccine to Effectively Stop cardio Thoracic Events and Decompensated heart failure (INVESTED) trial for participants from the Veterans Affairs healthcare system. Varying time windows around event dates were used to optimize events matching. The algorithms were externally validated for heart failure hospitalizations in the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC)-IV database.

### Results

We observed 100% sensitivity for death events with a 1-day window. Sensitivity for cardiovascular, heart failure, pulmonary, and nonspecific cardiopulmonary hospitalizations using discharge diagnosis codes varied between 75% and 95%. Including Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services data improved sensitivity with no meaningful decrease in specificity. The MIMIC-IV analysis showed 82% sensitivity and 99% specificity for heart failure hospitalizations.

### Discussion

EMR-based method accurately identifies all-cause mortality and demonstrates high accuracy for cardiopulmonary hospitalizations. This study underscores the importance of optimal time windows, data completeness, and domain variability in EMR systems.

### Conclusion

EMR-based methods are effective strategies for capturing death and hospitalizations in clinical trials; however, their effectiveness may be influenced by the complexity of events and domain variability across different EMR systems. Nonetheless, EMR-based methods can serve as a valuable complement to traditional methods.

**Keywords:** pragmatic clinical trials, electronic medical records, clinical endpoints, cardiovascular, pulmonary

## Introduction

Explanatory clinical trials, traditionally conducted as randomized clinical trials (RCTs) with restrictive entry criteria and intensive in-person procedures, currently represent the gold standard in rigorously testing investigational drug or device therapies.[1](#ocae303-B1),[2](#ocae303-B2) Recently, this design of trials is being modified as scientists seek to improve efficiency in the research ecosystem. The impetus for the overhaul is the unmet need to streamline the process of drug and device discovery, while limiting study cost and complexity.[3](#ocae303-B3) The inefficiency, costliness, and complexity of RCTs is multifactorial. Most notably, trials are time-consuming and resource-intensive, often conducted under controlled settings, and require extensive documentation and monitoring, research-trained study personnel, study-specific visits, and clinical endpoint adjudication.[4](#ocae303-B4) Additionally, data acquisition and patient-tracking remain problematic and burdensome. With an increased interest in optimizing study design, pragmatic clinical trials (PCTs) are gaining traction, especially in cardiovascular medicine.[5](#ocae303-B5) Unlike RCTs, which are conducted in optimal conditions, PCTs are designed to focus on effectiveness of an intervention in a real-world setting.[6](#ocae303-B6)

The increased utilization of electronic medical records (EMRs) worldwide and enrollment from centers with coordinated healthcare systems represent opportunities to reduce trial expenditures related to data acquisition and patient follow-up without impairing performance.[7](#ocae303-B7) Over the past decade, there has been a growing interest in investigating the agreement between centralized EMR and traditional methods of capturing endpoints and subsequent adjudication by clinical endpoints committees (CEC). An analysis of the WOSCOPS (West of Scotland Coronary Prevention Study) study showed excellent concordance between the death and hospitalization events captured in the clinical trial and routinely collected health records in Scotland.[8](#ocae303-B8) A post hoc analysis of the ASCEND trial has demonstrated the capability of EMR and registry death data in the United Kingdom to serve as an independent approach for certain cardiovascular events, eliminating the need for adjudication.[9](#ocae303-B9) Some studies have compared claims data or Hospital Episode Statistics with structured hospital record reviews or trial adjudicated events, both in the United States using Medicare data,[10](#ocae303-B10),[11](#ocae303-B11) and using Hospital Episode Statistics provided by English National Health Service hospitals.[12](#ocae303-B12) An analysis of the TRANSLATE-ACS (Treatment with Adenosine Diphosphate Receptor Inhibitors: Longitudinal Assessment of Treatment Patterns and Events After Acute Coronary Syndrome) study in the United States has reported lower rate of cumulative 1-year event rates when using medical claims to identify events compared to the traditional capture and CEC adjudication.[13](#ocae303-B13) However, Medicare and administrative claims data have been found to be comparable to adjudicated coronary outcomes, including all-cause mortality and certain procedural outcomes, in HiR (US Core Valve Pivotal High Risk) and SURTAVI (Surgical or Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement in Intermediate-Risk Patients) trials.[14–17](#ocae303-B14)

A recent review of cardiovascular studies involving 10 randomized trials from 1988 to 2016 has revealed high levels of agreement between routine health data and CEC adjudication methods for identifying all-cause mortality and cardiovascular outcomes,[18](#ocae303-B18) indicating the potential use of routine health data as an alternative for event adjudication in cardiovascular trials. This review has highlighted the importance of defining time windows for matching event dates between CEC and EMR methods, and that the decision to be restrictive or inclusive in the criteria for matching the dates can impact the completeness of capturing events due to inherent discrepancies in dating decisions between regions, systems, and over time. Completeness and accuracy of the routinely collected data as well as high linkage between the data sources in countries with a unified health system is reported as crucial for the accuracy of cardiovascular outcome detection and reliability of the outcome estimates.[8](#ocae303-B8),[18](#ocae303-B18)

The Veterans Affairs (VA) national healthcare system is well positioned to implement a pragmatic approach due to its EMR, scope, and coordinated care. The VA is the largest integrated healthcare system in the United States, offering care to over 9 million veterans.[19](#ocae303-B19) Using data captured from an international clinical trial, the INfluenza Vaccine to Effectively Stop cardio Thoracic Events and Decompensated heart failure (INVESTED), in which the VA was a major participant,[20](#ocae303-B20) this report is a secondary analysis that examines the comparability of centralized endpoint capture strategies in the EMR to traditional methods of event ascertainment. A team of clinicians, clinical researchers, experts on EMR-based phenotyping, and biostatisticians collaborated to develop and refine algorithms to identify the major clinical trial endpoints of cardiovascular and pulmonary hospitalizations and all-cause mortality outcomes in the VA EMR complemented by non-VA data sources. The sensitivity of event capture between the traditional and centralized methods is assessed for a sliding time window around the site-recorded event dates.

## Methods

### Study population

INVESTED was a prospective, double-blind, multi-site, randomized, active-controlled trial sponsored by the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) that compared high-dose (60 μg per viral strain) trivalent influenza vaccine and standard-dose (15 μg per viral strain) quadrivalent influenza vaccine for up to 3 influenza seasons and showed no difference between the 2 vaccine groups.[20](#ocae303-B20) The primary study registration identifier on ClinicalTrials.gov is [NCT02787044](https://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT02787044). The population for the current study is derived from the participants in the INVESTED trial, with 404 participants enrolled from the VA; due to the premature termination of the trial, we were not able to get the necessary approvals and consents to include the entire cohort of VA participants.

### INVESTED trial adjudicated events

The primary endpoint events in the INVESTED trial included all-cause mortality, cardiovascular hospitalizations (heart failure, cerebrovascular events, arrhythmias, and peripheral vascular disease), and pulmonary hospitalizations (pneumonia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease/chronic bronchitis, asthma, pulmonary embolism, and acute respiratory failure). These events were identified by local site efforts during 3 influenza seasons from 2016 to 2019 and formally reviewed by CEC. The CEC adjudicated the hospitalization endpoints into categories such as cardiovascular, pulmonary, non-cardiopulmonary, non-specific cardiopulmonary, or unknown; these CEC-adjudicated categories are considered the gold standard in this analysis. The definitions used by the CEC for these hospitalizations can be found in the [supplementary information](#sup1) of the INVESTED trial.[20](#ocae303-B20) A cardiopulmonary hospitalization was defined as any occurrence of cardiovascular or pulmonary hospitalization and if both reasons were present, it was classified as non-specific cardiopulmonary. Out of 477 trial hospitalization events for the 404 VA participants included in the current study, 54% were adjudicated as cardiovascular hospitalizations, 9% as pulmonary hospitalizations, 10% as non-specific cardiopulmonary hospitalizations, and the remaining as non-cardiopulmonary hospitalizations. For this analysis, we focus on all-cause mortality and cardiovascular, pulmonary, and non-specific cardiopulmonary hospitalization. Among individual categories of hospitalizations, we focus on heart failure hospitalizations alone since it was the only subcategory among hospitalizations with adequate representation to allow analysis.

### EMR events

A visual representation of the study workflow to compare these 2 approaches is provided in [Figure 1](#ocae303-F1). The VA EMR events were extracted by querying the VA Corporate Data Warehouse (CDW), a national VA data repository of over 1700 VA hospitals, clinics, and nursing homes. The CDW contains more than 60 domains that include demographics, diagnostic criteria including International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) diagnosis and procedure codes, and treatment information including Current Procedural Terminology codes for all VA inpatient and outpatient visits. The VA EMR data for the INVESTED cohort included in this study was searched for recorded deaths as well as non-fatal hospitalizations including those related to cardiovascular, pulmonary, and non-specific cardiopulmonary hospitalizations, which occurred during the trial period. Non-fatal hospitalization events are identified by their specified ICD codes and are extracted along with their event dates. To improve the quality of the EMR captured hospitalizations for each patient, we have also consolidated overlapping events with the same cause, the same provider ID, and the same admission room bed number. The ICD code groupings for cardiovascular and pulmonary diagnosis are given in the [Appendix (Table S1)](#sup1).

For identification of the hospitalizations in the VA EMR data, 2 general approaches are compared: the discharge diagnosis approach, which considers all diagnosis codes reported at patient’s discharge, and the principal diagnosis approach, which considers the one diagnosis code responsible for the longest length of patient’s stay. To enhance the completeness of event capture and explore the potential for improvements through combining VA and non-VA data, Purchased Care data (Fee data), which represents non-VA care paid for by the VA, and Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) data were also included in search for the events. In the initial step, the following 4 algorithms are compared: (a) Discharge diagnosis based on CDW data; (b) Discharge diagnosis based on CDW and Fee data; (c) Principal diagnosis based on CDW data; and (d) Principal diagnosis based on CDW and Fee data. In the subsequent step, CMS data were incorporated to assess their impact on the best selected algorithm based on CDW and Fee data. Although CMS data have certain limitations due to the Medicare age coverage being limited to individuals aged 65 years and older and its time lag in data release, we deemed it essential to include CMS data as a valuable healthcare data source in conjunction with other data sources. The present study’s analysis was conducted approximately 3 years after the INVESTED trial ended.

### Statistical methods

This study compares the centralized EMR-based method to the traditional method in terms of their sensitivity. Events are defined as a combination of patient ID and event date, and matching between the 2 methods is performed based on both criteria. The concordance between 2 hospitalizations is established by assessing whether, for the same patient, there is any overlap in the cause of the hospitalizations (a diagnosis match) and by verifying that the admission dates fall close enough to one another. In previous studies, a general 2-week time window around the event dates was used to account for the event time overlap.[8](#ocae303-B8),[16](#ocae303-B16) However, to find the best window, this study examines a reasonably wide range of sliding time windows around the admission dates, ranging from 0 days (having the same dates) to 50 days (being within at most 50 days of one another). This broader range allowed us to explore the effect of varying window sizes on sensitivity and ensure that we captured a wide variety of matching scenarios. It also aligns with our goal of optimizing EMR-based event capture methodologies in pragmatic trials. Hospitalizations for each participant were extracted per season starting from the participant's enrollment date up to the end of that season. As the size of the matching window increased, the period for extracting the CDW events was expanded to better compare the matching between the 2 methods. For example, for a window size of _w_, the CDW events for each participant in each season are extracted for a period of _w_ days prior to the participant’s enrollment date up to _w_ days after the end of that season.

In this study, we performed event-level comparisons rather than patient-level comparisons to evaluate and establish a robust methodology for centralized EMR-based capture of the clinical events. Sensitivity, which measures the ability of an approach to correctly identify true positive cases, was used to measure the ability of EMR-based approach in identifying the trial events for each hospitalization type and under different window sizes. Additionally, we employed specificity to gauge the EMR-based approach’s true negative identification, and positive predictive value (PPV) to represent the proportion of positive identifications that are indeed true positives. A higher sensitivity indicates a greater accuracy in detection of clinical trial events, while a higher specificity points to a greater accuracy in exclusion of non-events, and a higher PPV signifies a greater accuracy in positive event identifications.

### External validation

The MIMIC-IV database is one the largest publicly available, retrospectively collected medical databases, and it is sourced from the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center.[21–23](#ocae303-B21) The present study uses MIMIC-IV to validate the methodology described above in an external, publicly available dataset. While MIMIC-IV does not provide sufficient data to replicate the full analysis done on the INVESTED cohort, it includes DRG codes as well as ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes upon discharge for each patient encounter in the hospital. DRG codes were chosen as labels because they were the closest approximation to the VA EMR’s event adjudication.

Hospitalization events were filtered down to those labeled as HF as per DRG codes. In MIMIC-IV, each diagnosis code is not given a specific time beyond being connected to a hospitalization event, so the database lacks the same level of detail as the VA EMR events. Therefore, we did not have sufficient data to run the time window analysis on the MIMIC-IV dataset. Instead, we modified our approach to use either the primary ICD code or all ICD codes to determine HF status of each hospitalization. The set of all ICD codes in MIMIC-IV is equivalent to the set of discharge diagnosis codes for INVESTED, ensuring that the algorithms used for both datasets are equivalent. Sensitivity and specificity were computed when using the primary and all ICD codes upon discharge to predict encounters with heart failure DRG codes.

### Death events

A total of 17 deaths were recorded in the INVESTED trial among the 404 VA participants, all of which were matched with the EMR dataset. The event agreement rate is 94% for the same-date matches (16 events out of 17 events were matched with the same date) and 100% for a 1-day window.

### Hospitalization events

[Figure 2](#ocae303-F2) shows the sensitivity of the algorithms for cardiovascular, heart failure, pulmonary, and non-specific cardiopulmonary hospitalizations under different matching time windows around the event dates. Based on this comparison, discharge diagnosis approach had higher levels of sensitivity compared to the principal diagnosis approach. Also, the inclusion of Fee data as a non-VA data source does not seem to be a crucial determinant of success for either of these 2 algorithms. As the matching window changes from 0 to 5 days, the sensitivity of discharge diagnosis algorithm for cardiovascular hospitalizations increases from 81% to 84% and for heart failure hospitalizations from 88% to 91%. For pulmonary hospitalizations, the sensitivity ranges from 74% to 79% as the matching window changes from 0 to 10 days, and for non-specific cardiopulmonary hospitalizations, it ranges from 79% to 81% on changing the window from 0 to 1 day. As shown in [Figure 2](#ocae303-F2), adding the CMS data improved the sensitivity levels for all categories of hospitalizations. However, pulmonary and non-specific cardiopulmonary appear to benefit the most from adding the CMS data, likely because these conditions are more prevalent in older patients who often receive care outside the VA system, which is better captured in the CMS data.

### MIMIC-IV external validation

We analyzed 311 956 hospital encounters from 2008 to 2019, including those with and without heart failure. On average, using only the primary ICD-9 and ICD-10 code upon discharge to predict DRG codes resulted in a sensitivity of 82.3% and a specificity of 99.4%. For the same encounters, utilizing all discharge ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes increased sensitivity to 91.3% while decreasing specificity to 85.3%. For detailed values that can be used to compute sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and negative predictive value (NPV) for the external validation on MIMIC-IV, see the [Appendix (Tables S5 and S6)](#sup1).

## Discussion

Our study, comparing centralized capture of clinical trial endpoints from a national EMR supplemented with the Fee or CMS data with the traditional method of site-directed capture and adjudication by a CEC, demonstrated the validity of the EMR-based approach for mortality events and provided promising indications for its application to hospitalization events. In addition, our results also demonstrated the benefit of utilizing EMR for event capture, and the impact of time windows around a potential event for more accurate capture of events.

In our analyses, all-cause mortality events were completely captured using a 1-day window around the event dates. This corroborates the results of prior studies,[8](#ocae303-B8),[9](#ocae303-B9) suggesting that EMR-recorded death can be used as a standalone method of follow-up for trial mortality events. Furthermore, the analysis of non-fatal hospitalization events showed that EMR-based event capture is comparable to hospitalization events ascertained by laborious traditional procedures. The application of this method on 4 different categories of hospitalization events revealed that its effectiveness may be domain-dependent and inherent differences in the events or differences in the mechanisms for acquiring the event data in the EMR system, or completeness of the data sources, may affect the applicability of this approach. Traditional event capture may still be necessary for more complex events, particularly when data completeness and domain variability are concerns, due to the risks of false positives and misclassification errors. Still, EMR-based capture can serve as a valuable complement to traditional adjudication methods. This underscores the importance of carefully considering the specific context and design of each clinical trial when using EMR systems for capturing clinical trial endpoints.

This study has important implications for the future design and implementation of the EMR-based strategies to capture the clinical trial endpoints. The findings of this analysis suggest that discharge diagnosis codes, by detecting 75% to 95% of the trial adjudicated hospitalizations, could serve as a good foundation for developing EMR algorithms in the design of pragmatic trials. The corroboration of these results on the widely used MIMIC-IV suggests that these impacts on design and implementation are in no way limited to the VA healthcare system. However, the generalizability of these algorithms to other hospitals or countries with different coding practices or data limitations remains an area for future exploration. Evaluating how the algorithms perform in diverse healthcare systems with different coding standards, such as ICD-9, ICD-10, or national codes, will be important to ensure their adaptability and robustness in broader application. The observed levels of sensitivity and specificity underscore the EMR system’s capability to identify a substantial fraction of the adjudicated events. This suggests the utility of the EMR system as a preliminary filter to exclude a considerable portion of non-events, suggesting its intrinsic value in refining the adjudication process and bolstering the precision of event detection. The variation in PPV across different categories of events also offers promising directions for future research, such as the EMR approach’s potential to capture events possibly overlooked by adjudication.

In addition to potentially replacing traditional event capture and adjudication, EMR-based capture of hospitalization events could also be a valuable adjunct to CEC adjudication and used to trigger an event evaluation or to identify potential patients or events for the CEC to conduct a chart review, thereby reducing the burden on the site and the CEC. A post-hoc analysis of the PARADISE-MI (Prospective ARNI versus ACE Inhibitor Trial to Determine Superiority in Reducing Heart Failure Events after MI) trial showed intriguing results with implications for our study findings.[24](#ocae303-B24) The primary analysis of the study showed a non-significant benefit of the intervention, sacubitril-valsartan, when compared to ramipril. In a post hoc analysis, Pfeffer and colleagues showed that analysis using investigator-reported events showed a statistically significant benefit of the intervention.[25](#ocae303-B25)

In contrast to a static 2-week time window used for matching the events in previous studies,[8](#ocae303-B8),[16](#ocae303-B16) this study has additionally considered a varying time window in matching the hospitalization events. The findings of this analysis not only validate the previously suggested 2-week window but also demonstrate the impact of the size of matching time windows on accuracy of event capture.

This study highlights the potentials of using centralized EMR-based strategies as a key pragmatic methodology to improve efficiency of future trials. Pragmatic strategies offer numerous benefits,[26](#ocae303-B26) including reduced costs for patients and the clinical research ecosystem, improved timeliness of event reporting, detailed event capture, and streamlined trial participation for patients. Additionally, PCTs have the potential to benefit cardiovascular research by increasing the real-world applicability, improving patient engagement, and enrolling a more diverse patient population, which can be further explored in future research. Cardiovascular endpoints are not only important for CV trials, but they are also considered crucial to determine the safety of non-CV drugs such as anti-diabetics. Hence, our study demonstrates that an EMR-based approach to capture CV events is of sufficient accuracy compared to the cumbersome traditional approach and could advance the pragmatism of CV clinical trials.

### Limitations

While the study plan was to evaluate endpoints captured in all VA participants in the INVESTED trial, the early stoppage of the study prevented us from obtaining consent from all alive participants, limiting the study population to approximately 40% of enrolled Veterans or a cohort of 404.[20](#ocae303-B20) Furthermore, INVESTED’s premature termination prevented us from conducting additional analyses in the present study, such as a comparison of those receiving standard-dose and high-dose, due to a limited patient cohort. Nonetheless, the present study’s cohort size is within the standard range of similar work that conducts secondary analysis of clinical trials, especially in heart failure.[27–29](#ocae303-B27)

Since the inclusion of events for analysis was based on arbitrary local regulatory approvals, our results are unlikely to be affected by the restriction of the cohort analyzed. However, the smaller cohort size led to small numbers of specific events; hence, we were not able to perform detailed analysis of individual cardiovascular and pulmonary events except for heart failure hospitalizations. While our findings are based on the analysis of death and hospital admissions from a single study, our algorithms were developed to be widely applicable to other EMRs by predominantly using diagnostic codes. However, for other EMR systems and event types, specific algorithms may need to be developed by incorporating additional components, such as labs and medications, and potentially leveraging artificial intelligence.
