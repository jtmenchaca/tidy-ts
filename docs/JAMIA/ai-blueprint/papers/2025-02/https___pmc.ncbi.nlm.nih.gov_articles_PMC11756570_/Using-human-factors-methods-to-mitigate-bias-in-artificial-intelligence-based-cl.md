## Abstract

### Objectives

To highlight the often overlooked role of user interface (UI) design in mitigating bias in artificial intelligence (AI)-based clinical decision support (CDS).

### Materials and Methods

This perspective paper discusses the interdependency between AI-based algorithm development and UI design and proposes strategies for increasing the safety and efficacy of CDS.

### Results

The role of design in biasing user behavior is well documented in behavioral economics and other disciplines. We offer an example of how UI designs play a role in how bias manifests in our machine learning-based CDS development.

### Discussion

Much discussion on bias in AI revolves around data quality and algorithm design; less attention is given to how UI design can exacerbate or mitigate limitations of AI-based applications.

### Conclusion

This work highlights important considerations including the role of UI design in reinforcing/mitigating bias, human factors methods for identifying issues _before_ an application is released, and risk communication strategies.

**Keywords:** artificial intelligence, bias mitigation, clinical decision support, human factors methods

## Introduction

In this article, we discuss the interdependency between machine learning (ML)-based clinical decision support (CDS) and user interface (UI) design. We focus on 2 components of bias: the introduction of systematic error and factors that lead to a disproportionate weight in favor of or against something. One highly cited example is Google displaying fewer ads for high-paying jobs when the search engine thought the searcher was female.[1](#ocae291-B1) Although the systemic error that led to this result is not apparent to end users, the weight favoring men is clear. This is just one example. The growing use of algorithms and artificial intelligence (AI)-based applications has raised concerns of introduced or exacerbated discrimination and disparity, particularly in healthcare.[2–5](#ocae291-B2) Algorithms can introduce systematic errors stemming from homogenous training, choices made in modeling, bias in the existing data generation process, or any combination of these and other factors. These systematic errors can lead to prediction models that exacerbate inequities.

These sources of bias in AI are well-known and efforts are underway to develop strategies to reduce bias and unintended consequences.[6–9](#ocae291-B6) Solutions rely heavily on quantifiable definitions of fairness that are notoriously elusive. Indeed, data used to develop ML algorithms are often products of social and historical mechanisms that disadvantage some groups. As a result, available data may not properly represent disadvantaged or under-served populations, which makes it difficult to see bias in the measurement process.[6](#ocae291-B6)

AI tool developers create the best algorithms they can with available data, and implications of the developers’ assumptions are not always immediately apparent. For example, many ML risk assessment algorithms rely on administrative claims data that over represent people with medical insurance and underrepresent those without. In a widely used ML algorithm that used healthcare costs to identify patients with complex health needs, Black patients with more complex health needs such as hypertension and diabetes were less likely to be identified and receive support that would reduce their hospitalization risks than were healthier White patients.[10](#ocae291-B10)

Although every effort should be made to reduce these sources of bias, it is unlikely that truly unbiased data sets will ever exist. Even if an unbiased algorithm were developed, issues of how end-users would interpret and apply the application’s recommendations would remain. Thus, UI design is a critical and inter-related factor pertaining to bias, as the interface can lead clinicians to disproportionately weigh in favor for or against an idea or thing (ie, treatment recommendation, diagnosis, characterization of risk, etc.). There has been significant emphasis on algorithmic bias stemming from data/modeling choices; less attention has been paid to the potential for inequities to creep in during the translation of prediction model output to decision-making in clinical practice. One example is the use of default values in computerized order entry systems that have been found to significantly influence selection of laboratory tests, potentially leading to over testing.[11](#ocae291-B11)

We highlight 3 important considerations in designing UIs for AI applications: (1) Bias is not just about the algorithm; (2) It is possible to identify bias and interpretation errors _before_ an application is released; and (3) Risk communication strategies can influence bias in unexpected ways.

## Bias is not just about the algorithm

Consistent with principles of behavior economics, the design of the CDS UI influences how clinicians understand and act on AI-generated recommendations. Design choices can exacerbate or reduce the bias inherent in an algorithm. Designers must determine what to make salient and what to de-emphasize, make visible only via an additional click, or move to another screen altogether. User-feedback sessions are critically important to ensure a successful design is communicating as intended to end users.

Given the stakes, it is surprising that exploration of how users interpret information presented in a UI is often left out of design processes or considered as an afterthought when designs are nearly complete. Human-factors professionals recommend user-feedback sessions even at the conceptual stage—before a UI is developed.[12](#ocae291-B12),[13](#ocae291-B13) Early sessions explore clinicians’ understanding of the ML algorithm and its findings, interpretation of interface elements, and how intended designs support or hinder workflow. Wireframes and text-based scenarios can be used to present proposed interfaces and envisioned use cases. Users may be asked to describe their understanding of how the algorithm works, how they interpret representations of risk, what actions they would take when presented with sample recommendations, and when they might discount the recommendations. This type of user feedback is invaluable to designers if they are to create an interface that reduces misconceptions and bias. See Staes, Beck et al, 2024 for an excellent example.[14](#ocae291-B14),[15](#ocae291-B15)

Incorporating a series of in-depth one-on-one discussions with a small set of representative users into the production schedule can provide important insights that contribute to safe and effective health information technologies. Strategies for designing and testing UIs have been refined over time by the human factor community[16–19](#ocae291-B16) One essential strategy is sessions in which individual users are asked to imagine themselves using the proposed interface in a work context. Several use cases may be offered to encourage the user to consider strengths and limitations in different contexts. For example, a user might be asked to _imagine this alert appeared for a patient you have treated for many years_, and then asked to _imagine this alert appeared when you were seeing a colleague’s patient you do not know well_.

In addition to user-feedback sessions, heuristic evaluation and focus groups are often used as adjuncts, and sometimes replace user-feedback sessions. These strategies can identify usability issues but have important limitations. Heuristic reviews can be conducted relatively quickly as there are no end users to coordinate with, but the reviewers typically have little insight into how a clinician might interpret AI-based recommendations so they may miss important insights.[20](#ocae291-B20) Focus groups create an opportunity to obtain multiple end-user perspectives as facilitators lead the group through a discussion of candidate interface designs.[21](#ocae291-B21) The drawback is that efficiency is traded for depth. With multiple people in the discussion, there is less time for each to thoroughly explore and describe impressions and concerns.

Although recent strides have been made to better incorporate user feedback into software design processes, often design schedules do not allow adequate time to conduct these sessions or to redesign when issues are uncovered.[22](#ocae291-B22),[23](#ocae291-B23)

## It is possible to identify bias and interpretation errors before an application is released

An example based on the recent experience of the authors took place in the context of designing CDS for identifying patients at risk for opioid overdose. One early design concept indicated that a patient was at high risk for overdose with a red pointer on a risk scale ([Figure 1](#ocae291-F1)). In user-feedback sessions, some primary care clinicians reported that a patient flagged as high risk would be interpreted as a strong indicator that the patient should be referred to a pain management or addiction specialist. This is contrary to the intent. We intended to flag “high-risk” patients that would benefit from a careful re-evaluation of the benefits and risks of opioid analgesic use, a prescription for naloxone, and careful monitoring and assessment of other risk factors such as other concurrent medication use. For vulnerable patients, being forced to switch from a primary care clinician with whom they have a relationship to start afresh with a specialist would likely interrupt care continuity and might introduce other negative outcomes (eg, stigmatization). Patients who are historically disadvantaged may be penalized more than others as they may have financial or transportation barriers to obtaining specialty care. This early insight shaped the direction of our design. We moved toward describing the patient as having “elevated risk” rather than “high risk,” and eliminated the risk graphic ([Figure 2](#ocae291-F2)). The next iteration of user-feedback sessions explored designs that made recommended actions more salient, so that the risk statement was no longer the most noticeable element on the screen. Without early user testing, the UI design could have been implemented in a way that would have hampered the intended benefit of the CDS tool and disproportionally harmed historically disadvantaged patients. This is just one example of how UI design may exacerbate issues of bias in unexpected ways. Anecdotal reports from UI designers suggest that unexpected interpretations are routinely uncovered in user feedback sessions during development.

## Risk communication strategies can influence bias in unexpected ways

UIs may simply classify risk as high, medium, and low; include probabilities; or present statements that precisely describe an algorithm’s calculated risk. Although these characterizations may be accurate, they are often misinterpreted. Even highly educated clinicians in the fast-paced environment of patient care may struggle to properly interpret probabilities and risk statements, sometimes leading to unintended negative consequences, systematic errors, and bias.[24](#ocae291-B24),[25](#ocae291-B25)

For example, during the 1980’s AIDS epidemic, HIV-screening algorithms were designed to minimize missing a patient who was HIV positive. This increased the likelihood of false positives on initial screening and led to a bias toward premature diagnosis and treatment. The intent was that false positives were to be promptly identified in confirmatory testing. In reality, HIV-screening results were sometimes communicated to patients as certainties without considering testing-error rates. Misdiagnoses sometimes resulted in unnecessary aggressive treatment or suicide.[24](#ocae291-B24) Difficulties in understanding and communicating error probabilities and other types of risk are well-documented in spine surgery,[26](#ocae291-B26) cancer,[27](#ocae291-B27),[28](#ocae291-B28) and infectious disease.[29](#ocae291-B29)

Establishing the criteria for false positives and negatives is literally described as “setting the bias”; bias is inherent in these calculations. A clinical decision-support interface that does not make bias visible makes it difficult for clinicians to accurately interpret and convey uncertainty to patients. Thus, it is important to design interfaces that support users in understanding risks and biases associated with algorithm-derived recommendations, so they can use professional judgment to interpret and act.

How well UIs support clinicians in explaining risk to patients is another concern. Designing UIs to support informed shared decision-making in health care is notoriously challenging.[30–33](#ocae291-B30) For example, understanding frequency statements is easier than understanding probabilities. When told that one has a 30% to 50% chance of experiencing a sexual problem when taking an antidepressant, many people are uncertain about what this means. Some interpret this to mean they would experience this side effect in 30% to 50% of their sexual encounters. Restated as: “3 out of 10 people who take this medication will experience this sexual disfunction” reduces uncertainty.[24](#ocae291-B24)

In our efforts to design an opioid overdose risk prediction tool, we showed clinicians different representations of risk:

1.  High risk captures 75%-90% of patients who overdose.
    
2.  This patient is in the top 5th percentile of overdose risk.
    
3.  This patient has 10 times higher overdose risk than average.
    
4.  This patient has 2% chance of overdose.
    

Notably, all the representations were misunderstood by at least one clinician. This feedback inspired us to use an unambiguous frequency statement: _1 in 125 patients identified by this alert will experience an opioid overdose_.

We also created a figure to describe the error rates of the algorithm ([Figure 3](#ocae291-F3)). This figure visually depicts how the criteria are set: out of 10 000 patients, 10 will experience opioid overdose. Of those, the algorithm will correctly identify 4 patients and miss 6. Of the 9990 who will not experience an overdose, the algorithm will correctly exclude 9490 and incorrectly identify 500. The intent of this figure is to support clinicians in understanding the uncertainty associated with the recommendations generated by the algorithm, thereby reducing the bias toward referring all patients identified as high risk to a specialist.

## Conclusions

Bias in ML algorithms and AI-based CDS can be ameliorated or exacerbated by UI design. Although well-established human factors methods can be used to effectively address issues of bias and misuse of clinical-decision support, their use in this context is surprisingly rare. Often production schedules prevent user feedback sessions or only allow time for limited techniques such as focus groups and heuristic reviews. User-feedback sessions can help designers anticipate issues of bias, likely errors in interpreting recommendations, and potential unintended consequences. We recommend project leaders review human factors methods, and work with experienced human factors professionals to develop realistic timing and scope estimates during project planning.[34](#ocae291-B34),[35](#ocae291-B35)
