## Abstract

### Objective

Data extraction from the published literature is the most laborious step in conducting living systematic reviews (LSRs). We aim to build a generalizable, automated data extraction workflow leveraging large language models (LLMs) that mimics the real-world 2-reviewer process.

### Materials and Methods

A dataset of 10 trials (22 publications) from a published LSR was used, focusing on 23 variables related to trial, population, and outcomes data. The dataset was split into prompt development (_n_ = 5) and held-out test sets (_n_ = 17). GPT-4-turbo and Claude-3-Opus were used for data extraction. Responses from the 2 LLMs were considered concordant if they were the same for a given variable. The discordant responses from each LLM were provided to the other LLM for cross-critique. Accuracy, ie, the total number of correct responses divided by the total number of responses, was computed to assess performance.

### Results

In the prompt development set, 110 (96%) responses were concordant, achieving an accuracy of 0.99 against the gold standard. In the test set, 342 (87%) responses were concordant. The accuracy of the concordant responses was 0.94. The accuracy of the discordant responses was 0.41 for GPT-4-turbo and 0.50 for Claude-3-Opus. Of the 49 discordant responses, 25 (51%) became concordant after cross-critique, increasing accuracy to 0.76.

### Discussion

Concordant responses by the LLMs are likely to be accurate. In instances of discordant responses, cross-critique can further increase the accuracy.

### Conclusion

Large language models, when simulated in a collaborative, 2-reviewer workflow, can extract data with reasonable performance, enabling truly “living” systematic reviews.

**Keywords:** systematic review, meta-analysis, large language models, data extraction, natural language processing

## Introduction

As the medical literature expands at an unprecedented rate, physicians struggle to keep up with the latest evidence to inform their practice.[1](#ocae325-B1) Living systematic reviews (LSRs) address this challenge by continually integrating new findings and updating the evidence base.[2–9](#ocae325-B2) However, keeping systematic reviews living with manual screening of citations and data extraction is inefficient and impractical.[10](#ocae325-B10) Additionally, error rates in manual data extraction can reach up to 50%, resulting from the diverse backgrounds and varying statistical and clinical expertise of data abstractors[11](#ocae325-B11) underscoring the need of 2 reviewers to ensure reliability. However, manual data extraction is cost-intensive, adding to the challenge of maintaining LSRs in a truly “living” state.[12](#ocae325-B12) Automating the LSR workflow can enhance accuracy, reduce costs, and enable near-real-time evidence synthesis, addressing the critical bottleneck in maintaining LSRs’ timeliness.

Historically, natural language processing (NLP) techniques[13](#ocae325-B13) such as Latent Dirichlet Allocation, Conditional Random Fields, and support vector machines were used for text mining unstructured publication text.[14–18](#ocae325-B14) However, these models required extensively annotated datasets for training, consuming significant time and resources. The advent of large language models (LLMs) has revolutionized text generation and processing with Natural Language Instructions.[19](#ocae325-B19) LLMs, capable of tasks such as named entity recognition (NER), relation extraction, event identification, and text summarization,[20](#ocae325-B20) have been widely investigated for generalizable information extraction.[21](#ocae325-B21) Pretrained LLMs, enhanced through task-specific fine- and instruction-tuning,[22](#ocae325-B22) show diverse capabilities in data extraction and other systematic review tasks without requiring large volumes of annotated datasets for training.[23–26](#ocae325-B23) Notably, LLMs can critique and improve responses generated by other LLMs.[27](#ocae325-B27) While commercial LLMs pretrained on the general domain can perform unsupervised data extraction without further fine-tuning, various model-, data-, and framework-centric strategies can refine the response quality.[28](#ocae325-B28) Among these strategies, prompt engineering has emerged as an effective technique to condition LLM responses[29](#ocae325-B29) without the risk of overfitting associated with task-specific fine-tuning.[30](#ocae325-B30)

Given the unmet need for efficient data extraction for LSRs, we investigate a generalizable workflow ([GitHub Repository](https://github.com/CancerDataScience/LLMs_ExtractionWithCrossCritique)) leveraging Open AI’s GPT-4-turbo (GPT-4T)[31](#ocae325-B31) and Anthropic’s Claude-3-Opus (Claude-3O)[32](#ocae325-B32) to automate data extraction from clinical trial publications. The workflow employs systematically designed prompts in a zero-shot experimental design, mimicking the traditional 2-reviewer process in systematic reviews. We hypothesize that responses that are concordant between the LLMs are likely to be accurate, and in the instances of discordance, cross-critique between the LLMs will improve concordance.

## Background and significance

Utilizing LLMs for data extraction in systematic reviews and meta-analyses is an area of growing interest.[33](#ocae325-B33) Kartchner et al[34](#ocae325-B34) explored Open AI’s GPT-3.5-turbo and GPT-JT for summarizing and extracting information from clinical trials in 2 meta-analyses. In a zero-shot setting, GPT-3.5-turbo outperformed GPT-JT, however, its use was limited by a high degree of hallucinations within the generated responses. Gartlehner et al[35](#ocae325-B35) used Anthropic’s Claude-2 to extract data from 10 clinical trial publications, achieving an accuracy of 96.3%. However, the study was limited to categorical variables and clinical trials with only one or two arms. Konet et al[36](#ocae325-B36) compared GPT-4 and Claude-2 using the same publications and demonstrated a higher data extraction accuracy by GPT-4 (100%). However, this study focused on a few data elements, limiting the generalizability of their approach. Reason et al[37](#ocae325-B37) used GPT-4 for data extraction and code generation for network meta-analyses, achieving over 99% success by qualitative assessment. However, their approach relied only on publication text for data extraction and extracted data for a few variables, limiting its broader applicability.

Although these efforts showcase the promise of LLMs to automate data extraction for systematic reviews, existing efforts fall short by focusing on extracting high-level information that is insufficient for analyses, relying only on text to extract information, and demonstrating suboptimal performance. Utilizing a single LLM may generate responses with hallucinations, raising concerns about accuracy. We, therefore, investigate the collaborative LLM approach mimicking the traditional 2-reviewer LSR workflow for accurate data extraction.

## Methods

### Data sources

This study utilized data from a published LSR and network meta-analysis on first-line treatments for metastatic castration-sensitive prostate cancer.[3](#ocae325-B3) A manually curated gold-standard dataset derived from the full-text PDFs of 22 original and follow-up publications across 10 clinical trials was used to benchmark the data extraction performance of LLMs ([Table S1](#sup1)). The dataset included multiple publications for each clinical trial to account for selective outcome reporting and ensure comprehensive outcome reporting for the systematic review. The dataset included 23 variables categorized as “trial characteristics,” “population characteristics,” and “meta-analysis outcomes” for both the overall population and subgroups based on metastasis burden and timing ([Table S2](#sup1)).

### Data processing

The workflow of preprocessing clinical trial publications, data extraction, and postprocessing is shown in [Figure 1](#ocae325-F1). Each PDF was processed using rule-based programming with conventional Python libraries. Text from the publications was extracted using PyMuPDF.[38](#ocae325-B38) Nonessential sections, eg, “references,” “acknowledgments,” “appendices,” “ORCID IDs,” “author contributions,” “affiliations,” and “declaration of interests” were programmatically removed to focus on the relevant content and reduce the size of input tokens. The preprocessed text was split into smaller chunks based on GPT-4T and Claude-3O token limits to accommodate publications with longer text. Tiktoken library[39](#ocae325-B39) was used to manage token counts. To ensure continuity of context, a 1000-token overlap between chunks was maintained. Pages containing the tables and figures were identified by keywords (“table” and “figure”) and captured as images at 500 dots per inch (DPI) resolution.

### Sampling and prompting

The dataset was randomly sampled at the publication level and split into prompt development and test sets to prevent prompt overfitting[40](#ocae325-B40),[41](#ocae325-B41) and ensure generalization to new publications. The prompt development set was used for iterative prompt engineering to extract relevant information from text and images ([Figure S1](#sup1)). The test set was held out and not evaluated until the prompts were finalized.

Chunks of text and images from each PDF were independently processed for data extraction using the application programming interfaces (APIs) of GPT-4T (_gpt-4-0125-preview_ for text and _gpt-4-vision-preview_ for images) and Claude-3O (_claude-3-opus-20240229_), the latest available versions as of April 2024. The temperature of the models was set to zero to generate deterministic responses. Initially, a baseline prompt was used, which was iteratively refined based on the prompt development set performance ([Figure S1](#sup1)). The final prompts included the input text or image, required variables, and user-defined instructions for data extraction ([Methods S1](#sup1)).

### Postprocessing

Responses generated by GPT-4T and Claude-3O were processed separately. Extracted text and image responses were concatenated and processed using GPT-4T in a structured format ([Methods S1](#sup1)). The duplicate responses from different text chunks and images of the same publication were removed. Likewise, the most frequent response was selected for a given variable in case of conflicting responses from different text chunks and images. The postprocessing prompt included the extracted data and instructions for formatting.

### Concordance assessment and cross-critique

Two reviewers (M.A.K. and U.A.) assessed the concordance of the responses using a predefined criterion: responses from GPT-4T and Claude-3O were required to be exact matches or semantically similar for concordance. For discordant responses, the output of GPT-4T was provided to Claude-3O and vice versa for cross-critique. The cross-critique prompt included the original text or image, the other LLM’s response, and instructions to verify the response ([Methods S1](#sup1)). The responses after cross-critique were reassessed for concordance.

### Evaluation

Responses generated by GPT-4T and Claude-3O before and after concordance assessment and cross-critique were evaluated at the publication level for all variable categories. A sensitivity analysis was performed on the test set, excluding clinical trials that were also present in the prompt development set. Responses by LLMs were manually compared to the gold standard. Mean values with 95% CIs for accuracy, precision, recall, F1 scores, and percentage hallucinations were computed to assess performance. Hallucinations were defined as the non-N/A LLM responses that did not match the manually curated gold standard labels. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test[42](#ocae325-B42) was used to compare data extraction performance across different approaches, with a _P_\-value of <0.05 indicating a statistically significant difference in performance.

### Error analysis

An in-depth error analysis compared test set responses with hallucinations to the gold standard. Error categories were defined based on the nature of data extraction errors, and the hallucinated responses within each category were quantified.

### Data availability

The workflow for automated data extraction and the gold standard responses are available in a public repository ([GitHub Repository](https://github.com/CancerDataScience/LLMs_ExtractionWithCrossCritique)).

## Results

Ten clinical trials with 22 publications were included in this study ([Table S1](#sup1)). The publications were randomly split, with 5 (23%) in the prompt development set and 17 (77%) in the test set. A total of 277,810 text tokens (median: 11,434.5; _IQR: 9,588.3-15,462.3_) and 169 images (7.5; _6.3-9_) from the prompt development and test set publications were processed for data extraction. The prompt development set had 65,024 text tokens (median: 10,426; _IQR: 10,285-15,880_) and 42 images (9; _7-9_). The test set had 212,786 text tokens (median: 11,907; _IQR: 9,508-_14,209) and 127 images (7; _6-8_) ([Table S3](#sup1)).

The prompt development set had 115 responses (23 variables × 5 publications), of which 110 (96%) were concordant and 5 (4%) were discordant ([Figure S2](#sup1)). After cross-critique, 3 (60%) of the discordant responses became concordant, while 2 (40%) remained discordant. The test set had 391 responses (23 variables × 17 publications), of which 342 (87%) were concordant, and 49 (13%) were discordant. After cross-critique, 25 (51%) of the discordant responses became concordant, while 24 (49%) remained discordant. Details of the test set variables with discordant responses are shown in [Table S4](#sup1).

### Response evaluation in the single LLM approach

For 115 responses in the prompt development set, GPT-4T exhibited a mean accuracy of 0.95 (_95% CI: 0.93-0.97_), precision of 1.00, recall of 0.92 (_0.88-0.96_), and F1 score of 0.96 (_0.95-0.97_) whereas Claude-3O exhibited a mean accuracy of 0.95 (_0.93-0.97_), precision of 0.96 (_0.94-0.98_), recall of 0.97 (_0.95-0.99_), and F1 score of 0.96 (_0.94-0.98_). The evaluation of GPT-4T and Claude-3O by variable categories is reported in [Table S5](#sup1). Hallucinations were present in none (0%) of the responses with GPT-4T and 2.6% of the responses with Claude-3O. All Claude-3O responses with hallucinations were related to the extraction of meta-analysis outcomes. The percentage hallucinations of GPT-4T and Claude-3O by variable categories are reported in [Table S21](#sup1).

From 391 responses in the test set, GPT-4T exhibited a mean accuracy of 0.89 (_95% CI: 0.87-0.91_), precision of 0.98 (_0.96-1.00_), recall of 0.87 (_0.85-0.89_), and F1 score of 0.92 (_0.90-0.94_) whereas Claude-3O exhibited mean accuracy of 0.90 (_0.89-0.91_), precision of 0.94 (_0.92-0.96_), recall of 0.91 (_0.89-0.93_), and F1 score of 0.92 (_0.90-0.94_) ([Table S5](#sup1)). Hallucinations were present in 2.3% of the responses with GPT-4T and 2.8% of the responses with Claude-3O ([Table S21](#sup1)).

Sensitivity analyses showed GPT-4T to have a mean accuracy of 0.89 (_95% CI: 0.88-0.90_), precision of 0.98 (_0.97-0.99_), recall of 0.90 (_0.89-0.91_), and F1 score of 0.93 (_0.92-0.94_) and Claude-3O to have a mean accuracy of 0.92 (_95% CI 0.91-0.93_), precision of 0.94 (_0.93-0.95_), recall of 0.95 (_0.94-0.96_), and F1 score of 0.94 (_0.93-0.95_) ([Table S11](#sup1)). Hallucinations occurred in 2.0% of the responses with GPT-4T and 1.4% of the responses with Claude-3O ([Table S26](#sup1)). ### Error analysis

The LLM-generated responses with hallucinations were classified as errors due to the generation of fabricated responses, misinterpretation at the context level, interpretation at the variable level, and interpretation on ambiguity between more than one variable. The error categories are defined in [Figure S9](#sup1). The results show that the majority (62%) of hallucinations by GPT-4T were due to misinterpretation at the variable level ([Table S31](#sup1)). The majority (56%) of hallucinations by Claude-3O were due to misinterpretation at the context level, ie, extraction from the incorrect section of the document. After cross-critique, the majority (40%) of hallucinations by GPT-4T were due to the generation of fabricated responses, whereas the majority (64%) of hallucinations by Claude-3O were due to misinterpretation at the context level ([Table S32](#sup1)).

## Discussion

This study simulated the real-world setting for data extraction in systematic reviews and showed that LLMs, when used collaboratively, analogous to the 2-reviewer approach, demonstrate superior performance compared to the individual LLMs. The results of this study showed that the responses that are concordant between LLMs are likely to be correct. However, when they are discordant, cross-critique by LLMs, synonymous with human discussions while making decisions, further improves the accuracy. Given the rapid pace of evidence generation in domains like oncology[3–7](#ocae325-B3) and COVID-19,[43](#ocae325-B43) the laborious data extraction process was the bottleneck for creating truly “living” systematic reviews. Therefore, we have proposed a systematic LLM-based workflow ([Figure 4](#ocae325-F4)) to simulate a 2-reviewer setting that can be leveraged to facilitate the process of data extraction in living evidence synthesis informing clinical practice guidelines.

The proposed approach has several strengths. First, the collaborative LLM approach reduces the risk of hallucinated responses, which is a major limitation of using individual LLMs in the real-world practice of systematic reviews. This is evident by a higher mean test set accuracy of 0.94 with collaborative LLMs compared to GPT-4T (0.89) and Claude-3O (0.90) alone ([Figure 2](#ocae325-F2)). Likewise, the subset of responses with the collaborative LLM approach had fewer hallucinations (0.25%) compared to individual LLMs (∼2.5%). Second, we have developed a templated prompt specifically for data extraction that can be modified to extract different sets of variables across a variety of published articles ([Figure S1](#sup1)). This enables the reviewers to incorporate new variables in an existing LSR or to initiate a new review, making the workflow generalizable. Third, this approach offers significant time and cost savings. The average cost for data extraction was $3.40 for one publication, with an additional cost of $2.06 for cross-critique in almost real-time. Given these findings, this approach would significantly lower the costs and tremendously expedite the data extraction in LSR which otherwise would take months of manual effort.[10](#ocae325-B10),[12](#ocae325-B12)

However, the findings of this study should be interpreted in the context of some limitations. The dataset used in this study includes only prostate cancer trials and hence, the applicability to the fields outside oncology may be limited. However, we followed a systematic approach by randomly sampling our dataset into prompt development and test sets for unbiased evaluation. It could be argued that the inclusion of different publications from the same clinical trials in both prompt development and test sets could potentially contaminate the test data. However, a sensitivity analysis including unique clinical trials with no publications present in the prompt development set showed consistent results. In addition, using 2 instances of the best-performing LLM rather than 2 different LLMs could have led to better overall performance. However, this study utilized different LLMs since each model has distinct parametric knowledge and patterns of understanding and processing language, providing complementary perspectives similar to human reviewers. Although this study utilized 2 LLMs, using more than 2 LLMs could enhance performance. However, we anticipate that any increase in performance with using more than 2 LLMs would occur at the expense of significantly increased computational cost. It is also possible that the LLMs were previously exposed to publications that were included in the prompt development and test sets, which could have potentially overestimated the performance.[44](#ocae325-B44) While the collaborative approach achieved perfect accuracy for extracting variables like trial characteristics, the performance was relatively lower for other variables where implicit judgment is required, highlighting the need for more intelligent approaches for achieving automation. Integrating a third independent or dependent reviewer to resolve discrepancies can offer additional insights into the collaborative performance of LLMs ([Figure 4](#ocae325-F4)). Other potential avenues to improve performance and minimize hallucinations include self-consistency, ie, generating multiple responses to the same prompt at different temperature settings and selecting responses that appear most consistently across different outputs.

In the future, we aim to integrate the proposed approach into our existing living interactive evidence (LIvE) synthesis framework[45](#ocae325-B45) with a focus on reducing manual effort by iteratively identifying areas where LLMs can autonomously handle data synthesis tasks ([Figure 4](#ocae325-F4)). Given the promising performance of the collaborative LLMs approach in this study, there is significant potential for its integration into both existing and new living evidence infrastructures.[3–7](#ocae325-B3),[46–57](#ocae325-B46) Beyond its immediate application in data extraction, this approach could extend to other critical steps in the systematic review process, such as screening, quality assessment, and evaluating the certainty of evidence.[58](#ocae325-B58) Additionally, the LLMs can be used collaboratively as planners for designing sophisticated analytic strategies enabling advanced meta-analysis.[59](#ocae325-B59),[60](#ocae325-B60) LLMs that can directly process PDFs at scale are now emerging and can potentially be used without preprocessing text from PDFs. Likewise, with improving text processing capabilities of data in different formats such as HTML, a future direction involves LLMs to directly extract data from the available online manuscript versions on publication websites. Moreover, a key future direction involves further exploring the reasoning chains of LLMs, which could not only enhance confidence in their use but also improve trackability throughout the data extraction process. Beyond evidence synthesis, the collaborative LLM approach can be integrated into clinical workflows and systems to advance patient care and quality improvement initiatives. Developing guidelines and safeguards for using LLMs in evidence synthesis and healthcare is essential for widespread adoption. Training healthcare professionals and researchers to use AI-based tools effectively will be crucial for successfully implementing these solutions.

## Conclusions

In summary, concordant responses by the LLMs are likely to be accurate. In instances of discordant responses, cross-critique can further increase the accuracy. LLMs, when simulated in a collaborative, 2-reviewer workflow, can extract data with reasonable performance, enabling truly “living” systematic reviews. Validation of this workflow on external datasets is required to assess its widespread applicability.
