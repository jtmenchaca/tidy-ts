## Abstract

### Objective

To describe the prevalence of and trends in practices that interfere with the exchange of patient health information (potential information blocking) 2 years after implementation of information-blocking regulations.

### Materials and Methods

Drawing from the American Hospital Association Information Technology (IT) Supplement and a national survey of health information organizations (HIOs), we described rates and methods of potential information blocking from these organizations’ perspectives in 2023 and compared them to prior years.

### Results

Twenty-seven percent of hospitals sometimes or often observed potential information blocking by any actor in 2023, down from 42% in 2021 and 33% in 2022. Thirty percent of HIOs routinely observed potential information blocking by health IT developers, down from 50% in 2015. 13% of HIOs routinely observed potential information blocking by hospitals and health systems, down from 25% in 2015. According to both hospitals and HIOs, the most prevalent method of potential information blocking by developers in 2023 was through price, while the most prevalent by healthcare providers/health systems was by focusing exchange on strategic affiliations. Few hospitals and HIOs that experienced potential information blocking said that they had reported it to the Department of Health and Human Services.

### Discussion

Hospitals and HIOs perceived lower rates of potential information blocking in 2023 than in prior years indicating some impact of regulations addressing information blocking. However, both respondent types reported that substantial potential information blocking persisted in 2023 and negatively impacted the exchange of information.

### Conclusion

While potential information-blocking practices have decreased, they have not been eliminated, indicating the value of continued and robust enforcement of information-blocking regulations.

**Keywords:** interoperability, hospitals, health system, health information exchange

## Background and significance

In 2016, Congress passed the 21st Century Cures Act (Cures Act), which included a provision that addresses “information blocking,” defined in part as a practice that is likely to interfere with the access, exchange, or use of electronic health information (EHI). The Assistant Secretary for Technology Policy/Office of the National Coordinator for Health Information Technology (hereafter ASTP) published the Cures Act Final Rule on May 1, 2020, with an applicability date in April 2021 for the information-blocking regulations.[1](#ocaf007-B1) HHS continues to update information-blocking policies through subsequent rulemakings.[2–5](#ocaf007-B2) As of August 2024, no information-blocking actor has been subject to a civil monetary penalty or disincentive under the Cures Act’s information-blocking provision.[6](#ocaf007-B6)

In a 2015 report requested by Congress, ASTP described information blocking as a concern because of its potential to inhibit the development and implementation of safe interoperable health information technology that supports patient care while advancing research and public health.[7](#ocaf007-B7) Stopping information blocking could therefore increase the safety, effectiveness, and efficiency of the healthcare delivery system by ensuring that a patient’s medical history is shared across organizations when the patient receives care from multiple providers. Research before and after the Cures Act has identified both incentives and barriers that shape how healthcare organizations engage in the exchange of health information across organizations. That research has highlighted that hospitals,[8](#ocaf007-B8) skilled nursing facilities,[9](#ocaf007-B9),[10](#ocaf007-B10) regional, state, and local health information exchange organizations (HIOs),[11](#ocaf007-B11) ambulatory clinics,[12](#ocaf007-B12) and primary care physicians[13](#ocaf007-B13) each report unique challenges with accessing, exchanging, and using EHI. It has also highlighted that healthcare organizations and developers of certified health information technology (IT) may have competitive incentives to avoid facilitating the easy exchange of patient health information, but the presence of these incentives does not directly imply these organizations are engaged in information blocking.[14–18](#ocaf007-B14)

Given this complex landscape, ASTP noted in the 2015 report to Congress on information blocking that, relative to other barriers and facilitators, “the extent to which information blocking is impeding the effective sharing of EHI is less clear” and that “formal research is limited and anecdotal evidence is often difficult to interpret and still more difficult to generalize.”[7](#ocaf007-B7) There have been only a handful of reports addressing this gap in the years since the Cures Act was passed. A survey of HIOs conducted in 2015 showed that 50% of HIOs believed that Electronic Health Record (EHR) developers routinely engaged in information blocking and 25% of HIOs believed that hospitals and health systems routinely engaged in information blocking.[19](#ocaf007-B19) An updated survey in 2019 showed that 56% of HIOs believed that some, most, or all EHR developers engaged in information blocking and 24% said the same about hospitals and health systems.[20](#ocaf007-B20) A survey of hospital leaders in 2021 showed that 42% sometimes or often observed practices that may constitute information blocking[21](#ocaf007-B21) and an update the following year showed that just 32% of hospital leaders—10 percentage points fewer–reported sometimes or often observing information blocking.[22](#ocaf007-B22) ASTP also maintains a database of complaints that it has received, but tabulations of those complaints reveal what is reported to ASTP and may not reflect a balanced view of the impact or prevalence of information blocking.[23](#ocaf007-B23)

Given the limited (and in some cases outdated) data on information blocking, additional and updated information focused on change over time could be useful to develop a robust picture of the extent of activities that may constitute information blocking following the implementation of information-blocking regulations and industry’s response.

## Objective

We aimed to update and reinforce the available data on the prevalence of potential information blocking through simultaneous surveys of hospitals and HIOs. This approach is based on the principle of data triangulation—using multiple data sources to identify similar and diverging patterns and increase confidence in findings.[24](#ocaf007-B24),[25](#ocaf007-B25) By describing simultaneous surveys, we aimed to achieve 3 specific objectives: (1) credibly identify trends in perceived information blocking 2 years after the initial effective date of the Cures Rule, (2) robustly assess the prevalence of information blocking from various actors and by the specific type or method of information blocking, and (3) identify hospital and HIO views on information blocking that may be idiosyncratic to one set of actors and not representative of the delivery system overall.

## Materials and methods

### Data

Data on hospitals’ perspectives on information blocking came from the IT [Supplement](#sup1) to the American Hospital Association (AHA) Annual Survey for survey years 2021, 2022, and 2023. The IT [supplement](#sup1) is sent annually to all US hospital Chief Executive Officers as represented within the AHA Annual Survey and completed by Chief Information Officers (CIOs) or the person most knowledgeable about the hospital’s use of health IT. Development of the IT [Supplement](#sup1) content is a collaboration between the AHA and the Assistant Secretary for Technology Policy; it has been fielded annually since 2008 to track hospital adoption and use of health information technology. The IT [supplement](#sup1) contains approximately 40 questions on hospital use of and experience with health IT and is fielded by the AHA separately from the Annual Survey. In 2023, the survey was fielded from March to August 2023, in 2022 the survey was fielded from July to December, and in 2021 the survey was fielded from April to September. Hospitals were contacted multiple times by the AHA, but were not offered compensation to respond.

We included all responses from non-federal acute care hospitals in the United States, the population of hospitals that received Meaningful Use subsidies and therefore have comparable experience with health IT. We included all hospitals that responded to at least one question on information blocking each year so that response rates to specific questions varied. 2419 non-federal acute care US hospitals responded to at least one question related to information blocking on the IT [supplement](#sup1) in 2023 (response rate = 55%), 2446 hospitals responded in 2022 (response rate = 55%), and 2092 hospitals responded in 2021 (response rate = 47%).

Data on HIOs’ perspectives on information blocking came from a national survey of HIOs conducted by the University of California, San Francisco, in collaboration with ASTP and the Civitas Networks for Health, a national collaborative of HIOs for survey years 2015, 2019, and 2023. We recently described the process for fielding this survey elsewhere.[26](#ocaf007-B26) In 2023, 90 identified HIOs met the inclusion criteria (supported live EHI exchange across unaffiliated entities as of March 1, 2022) and 76 responded (84% response rate). In 2019, 106 organizations met inclusion criteria and 89 responded (84% response rate). In 2015, 105 organizations met inclusion criteria and 60 responded (57% response rate).

#### Trends in frequency of potential information blocking

Since 2021, hospitals have been asked to report the extent to which they perceive “Developers of Certified Health IT (eg, Enterprise EHR Developers),” “National Networks (eg, CommonWell, eHealth Exchange),” “State, regional, and/or local health information exchange,” and “Healthcare providers” engage in potential information blocking. Response options were “Often/Routinely,” “Sometimes,” “Never/Rarely,” or “Do not know.” We compared the fraction of respondents that indicated that they “Sometimes” or “Often/Routinely” observed potential information blocking by the 4 listed entity types and by any of the 4, in each year.

HIOs were similarly asked about the perceived frequency of potential information blocking in 2015, 2019 and 2023. In 2019 and 2023, HIOs were separately asked what proportion of (1) EHR Vendors and (2) Hospitals and Health Systems they encountered engaging in potential information blocking. Response options were “Few,” “Some,” “Most,” or “All.” We trended the proportion of HIOs that indicated that some, most or all of each entity engaged in potential information blocking. In 2019 and 2023, HIOs that reported encountering at least a few of each entity engaging in potential information blocking were then asked how often that entity engaged in potential information blocking, while all HIOs were asked about frequency in 2015. Due to changes to response options in each fielding, only the response for “Routinely” was consistent in all 3 surveys. We therefore trended the percent of HIOs that indicated that health systems or EHR Vendors routinely engaged in potential information blocking in 2015, 2019, and 2023.

To generate roughly comparable metrics between hospitals and HIOs, we trended the proportion of hospitals that indicated often/routinely observing potential information blocking by developers of certified health IT (similar to EHR Vendors) and healthcare providers (similar to health systems). We report these results in the Appendix S1.

#### Methods used to engage in potential information blocking

Both HIOs (in 2019 and 2023) and hospitals (in 2021, 2022, and 2023) were asked how often they had observed or experienced EHR Vendors and healthcare providers using specific methods to engage in information blocking. For EHR Vendors, those methods were (1) price, (2) contract language, (3) artificial technical, process, or resource barriers, and (4) refusal. For healthcare providers, those methods were (1) artificial technical, process, or resource barriers, (2) refusal, or (3) strategic affiliations (described as closed-network exchange on the HIO survey and only included in 2023). The survey instruments, which included illustrative examples, are included as [Supplementary material S1](#sup1).

In total, 14 metrics related to methods of information blocking were reported in 2023, with 7 reported by HIOs and 7 reported by hospitals. We tabulated relative rates of each method, as observed by HIOs and hospitals in 2023. Data were available for multiple years for 13 of these 14 metrics each of these metrics. We trended the proportion of hospitals and HIOs that reported “Sometimes” or “Often/Routinely” observing each method of information blocking for these metrics.

#### Information blocking by specific actors

In addition to questions about EHR Vendors and hospitals and health systems, HIOs were asked to what extent they observed potential information blocking among other specific actors including (1) commercial payers, (2) commercial laboratories, (3) commercial pharmacies, (4) national networks, (5) state, regional, and/or local health information exchanges. We compared the proportion of HIOs that reported each “Often/Routinely” engaged in potential information blocking to rates reported by HIOs for EHR Vendors and hospitals and health systems.

#### Impact of potential information blocking on HIOs’ services

In 2023, in addition to reporting the frequency and methods of potential information blocking, HIOs were asked to report on the degree to which potential information blocking impacted their ability to effectively provide patient information to diverse organizations in support of effective treatment. Specifically, HIOs were asked “To what extent does information blocking by hospitals and health systems lead to missing patient health information?” and “To what extent does information blocking by EHR Vendors make it more difficult for you to provide HIE services to your participants?” Response options for each question were “Greatly,” “Moderately,” “Minimally/Not at all,” or “Don’t know.”

We tabulated HIOs’ responses to these questions to provide an overall view of the impact of information blocking on the ability to share information between organizations.

#### Frequency of reporting potential information blocking

Both hospitals and HIOs were asked about whether they reported information blocking for potential investigation. HIOs were asked, “When you have experienced practices that you believed constituted information blocking by EHR Vendors in the past year, how often did you report the information blocking to ONC/HHS?” and could report “Always,” “Most of the time,” “Sometimes,” “Rarely,” or “Never.” Hospitals were asked, “If you have experienced practices that you believed constituted information blocking in the past year, how often did you report the information blocking to ONC/HHS using the Report Information Blocking Portal?” and could respond “Often/Routinely,” “Sometimes,” “Never/Rarely,” “Did not experience information blocking within the past year,” or “Do not know”.

We aligned HIO and hospital response options and tabulated their responses to describe and compare the frequency with which each entity reported potential information blocking.

### Analytic approach

Where direct comparisons of rates are made across years, respondents, or methods of potential information blocking, we used Rao-Scott corrected chi-square tests to examine whether differences were statistically significant. In these tests, we implemented finite population correction because both hospital and HIO respondents represented a substantial portion of the population. We also implemented non-response weights to generate statistics representative of all US hospitals. We generated probabilities using a logistic regression to predict the likelihood that a hospital in the full AHA Annual Survey responded to the IT [Supplement](#sup1) based on the hospital’s size, ownership, teaching status, system membership, and availability of a cardiac intensive care unit, urban status, and region.

This study was exempt from institutional review board review as it uses organization-level survey data. We used Stata 15 SE for all statistical analyses and a cutoff of _P_ = .05 to determine statistical significance.

## Results

### Trends in frequency of potential information blocking

The percentage of hospitals that reported observing any entity engaging in potential information blocking declined from 42% in 2021 to 27% in 2023 ([Figure 1A](#ocaf007-F1); χ2 statistic: 56.2; _P_ < .001). This decline was driven by a 24-percentage point decrease in hospitals that reported potential information blocking by healthcare providers between 2022 and 2021 (χ2 statistic: 291.4; _P_ < .001). The downward trend continued between 2022 and 2023, primarily due to a decrease in the proportion of hospitals that reported experiencing potential information blocking by developers of certified health IT from 22% in 2022 to 12% in 2023 after a brief increase from 17% in 2021 to 22% in 2022 (χ2 statistic: 72.3; _P_ < .001).

The percentage of HIOs that reported observing at least some EHR Vendors engaging in potential information blocking decreased from 49% in 2019 to 26% in 2023 ([Figure 1B](#ocaf007-F1); χ2 statistic: 42.6; _P_ < .001). Similarly, the percentage of HIOs that observed at least some hospitals and health systems engaging in potential information blocking decreased from 22% in 2019 to 10% in 2023 (χ2 statistic: 18.0; _P_ < .009).

The percent of HIOs that reported observing EHR Vendors routinely engaging in potential information blocking decreased from 50% in 2015 to 30% in 2023 ([Figure 1C](#ocaf007-F1); χ2 statistic: 10.8; _P_ < .001), far above the 2% of hospitals that reported that developers of certified health IT routinely engaged in potential information blocking ([Appendix Figure S1](#sup1)). The percent of HIOs that reported experiencing health systems routinely engaging in potential information blocking similarly decreased from 25% to 13% (χ2 statistic: 4.8; _P_ = .009) and remained far above the 1% of hospitals that reported observing healthcare providers routinely engaging in potential information blocking.

### Trends in methods used to engage in potential information blocking

There was an absolute decline for 11 of 13 measured methods of potential information blocking across actors and respondents ([Figure 2](#ocaf007-F2)). The only 2 measured methods that did not decline over time were hospitals’ observation of developers of certified health IT refusing to exchange information or using contract language to engage in potential information blocking.

### Methods used to engage in potential information blocking

HIOs reported observing each method of potential information blocking more commonly than hospitals ([Figure 3](#ocaf007-F3)). However, both HIOs and hospitals reported that the most common method EHR Vendors and developers of certified health IT used to engage in perceived potential information blocking was through high price (reported by 60% of HIOs and 24% of hospitals \[χ2 statistic: 28.6; _P_ < .001\]) and the least common method was through refusal (reported by 32% of HIOs and 11% of hospitals \[χ2 statistic: 18.4; _P_ < .001\]). Similarly, both HIOs and hospitals reported that healthcare providers most often engaged in potential information blocking through strategic limits: HIOs reported that the most common method health systems used to engage in information blocking was through closed-network exchange (reported by 45% of HIOs) and hospitals reported that the most common method healthcare providers used to engage in potential information blocking was by limiting exchange based on strategic affiliation (reported by 22% of hospitals). HIOs and hospitals differed most on their assessment of how often health systems or healthcare providers engaged in potential information blocking through refusal, with 40% of HIOs reporting observing this method compared to just 9% of hospitals (χ2 statistic: 47.6; _P_ < .001).

### Frequency of reporting information blocking

Only a small minority of respondents—8% of HIOs and 3% of hospitals—that had experienced potential information blocking reported that they had submitted a claim to the ASTP “report information blocking” portal, a mechanism for the public to report claims of possible information blocking ([Figure 6](#ocaf007-F6)).

## Discussion

The prevalence and impact of information blocking have been challenging to measure empirically. For an actor’s practice (act or omission) to be “information blocking” as defined in the information-blocking regulations, it must be done with a certain level of knowledge by the actor and likely to interfere with the access, exchange, or use of EHI–without being required by law or covered by an information-blocking exception.[27](#ocaf007-B27) Whether any particular practice (an act or omission) by an actor constitutes information blocking depends on the unique facts and circumstances of the practice.[28](#ocaf007-B28) Therefore, it can be difficult to disentangle from other sources of friction that may limit health information exchange. By presenting parallel data from hospitals and HIOs, we highlight that the relevance of potential information blocking logically varies across entities while triangulating common themes across organizations with substantially different roles in information exchange. Despite differences in their perceived overall prevalence of potential information blocking, both HIOs and hospitals report that potential information blocking has declined and that, nevertheless, substantial levels of potential information blocking persisted in 2023.

The decline reported by both hospitals and HIOs in potential information blocking provides encouraging evidence that information-blocking actors may be adjusting their practices in response to the passage of the 21st Century Cures Act and regulations issued under it. The data reveal idiosyncratic trends in behavior change that, we speculate, likely reflect anticipatory behavior change, differing attention to legal and regulatory changes, and the interwoven nature of interoperable information exchange. For instance, it appears that developers changed behavior following the Cures Act—that is—between 2015 and 2019, resulting in a substantial decrease in the proportion of HIOs that reported routinely observing information blocking by developers. In contrast, healthcare providers may have reacted to the implementation of regulation, effective in 2021, resulting in a decrease in the proportion of hospitals reporting information blocking by healthcare providers between 2021 and 2022. Subsequent decreases in the proportion of hospitals reporting that developers engaged in information blocking could have been a second order effect of provider behavior change and a response to changing customer demands.

Despite these idiosyncrasies, the parallel declines noted in potential information blocking by healthcare providers, headlined by a large decrease in refusing to engage in information exchange, highlight an important shift in how these actors approach interoperability. It is also notable that the decrease in potential information blocking has been relatively broad across methods of potential information blocking and that no method has increased over time—that is, there does not appear to be a shift from one, perhaps more obvious, method of information blocking, like refusal, to another that would be harder to define or identify, like the presence of artificial barriers. Taken together, these results indicate a general trend of improved access, exchange, and use of EHI among information-blocking actors in the last several years.

However, while the reported prevalence of potential information blocking appears to be declining, its absolute prevalence remains substantial, with 27% of hospitals in 2023 reporting sometimes or often observing potential information blocking from any actor, 45% of HIOs reporting that potential information-blocking practices by health systems “greatly” or “moderately” resulted in missing information, and 61% of HIOs reporting that potential information blocking by developers “greatly” or “moderately” created difficulty providing services to their customers. There may be several reasons why hospitals and HIOs reported observing potential information blocking at these levels in 2023, including lack of awareness of the regulations, enforcement mechanisms that had not been implemented prior to the survey, and market dynamics that make changing behavior difficult. It is also likely that some behaviors respondents believed constitute information blocking reflect other sources of continued friction in the exchange of patient health information. For instance, the 2 most common methods of potential information blocking that HIOs reported for developers, price and artificial barriers, seem hard for an outside entity seeking to exchange data with a health IT developer to parse from legitimate costs and barriers. Of course, any instance of potential information blocking would require a fact-specific investigation that takes into account all the elements of the information blocking definition.[29](#ocaf007-B29)

The differences in the frequency of observed potential information blocking between hospitals and HIOs may demonstrate that organizations’ risk or exposure to information blocking differs depending on where one “sits” in healthcare. For example, it may be that healthcare providers feel more able to simply refuse to exchange information with an HIO than a hospital, leading to higher rates of potential information blocking through “refusal” encountered by HIOs compared to hospitals. Similarly, HIOs may report more artificial barriers by developers due to HIOs’ function as a hub for information sharing, and the fact that they likely work with a wider range of developers than a particular hospital. To this point, we observed differences across hospital and HIO characteristics in prior analyses indicating the importance of competition and hospital size and ownership in how they perceived information blocking.[20](#ocaf007-B20),[21](#ocaf007-B21) Additional data on the experience of potential information blocking by a wider range of entities (eg, independent physician practices, long-term care providers), could be important to identify particular pain points where information blocking is most acute.

Given these differences, ameliorating the frictions that still exist likely requires an understanding of the relationships and incentives at play in a specific area of the healthcare system, which seems to discourage a one-size-fits-all approach and supports the facts- and circumstances-based enforcement of information blocking. This enforcement paradigm is relatively common: for example, it is employed in determining whether the use of telehealth services was in good faith and investigations into security breaches and impermissible disclosure of PHI.[30](#ocaf007-B30),[31](#ocaf007-B31) While these data are supportive of an enforcement approach that allows for the careful consideration of relevant facts and circumstances in individual cases, that enforcement paradigm does create potential practical and resource challenges that will likely shape the number and type of enforcement activities undertaken by the HHS Office of the Inspector General (OIG), for instance, by focusing on enforcement likely to drive the broadest market change.

Among surveyed HIOs and hospitals, 92% and 97%, respectively, reported that they never or rarely send reports of potential information blocking to HHS. If these figures are directionally correct across various types of other organizations in the healthcare system, it could indicate that there is underreporting of behavior that entities believe could potentially constitute information blocking, and that the behavior which is reported may not be representative of the full scope of what is occurring. Underreporting seems likely to be driven by limited incentives to report, particularly because OIG has not yet pursued enforcement against an organization. It may also relate to the potential for reprisal from important business partners that an organization reports despite assurances of confidentiality from HHS, or simply lacking knowledge of how and when to report. Continued educational efforts and initial enforcement actions by OIG could alter this dynamic.

Whatever the cause, underreporting and selection effect inform our view of publicly available data about information-blocking claims, such as the data released by ASTP in a _Quick Stat_, updated monthly.[23](#ocaf007-B23) To date, most of the claims reported in the _Quick Stat_ appear to have been submitted by patients and to involve healthcare providers. It appears that at least a segment of the patient population is aware of the information-blocking regulations and has been sufficiently motivated to submit claims to HHS regarding potential information blocking. This presents an interesting contrast with our data from surveys of hospitals and HIOs, in which developers are more commonly cited than healthcare providers in terms of friction with health information exchange. While this difference likely indicates that patients are more often directly exposed to potential information blocking from providers than developers, it likely also indicates that certain types of potential information blocking are under-reported relative to their prevalence.

If there is a selection effect occurring on who is likely to submit an information-blocking claim to HHS, then HHS may need to continue to gather reports and feedback from a variety of entities across the healthcare system to assess the scope and type of continued behavior that could constitute information blocking. HHS may also consider the prevalence of potential information-blocking actions in this data as a [supplement](#sup1) to existing priorities and choose to pursue cases that reflect more prevalent methods of potential information blocking.[32](#ocaf007-B32)

This study indicates areas of useful research to understand information-blocking behaviors. First, understanding individual or organizational dynamics that capture different actors’ incentives, pain points, and relationships, and how their interaction leads to (or prevents) potential information blocking could lead to a framework to describe information blocking. Second, evidence on how actors changed their workflows in response to the Cures Act and the information-blocking regulations could inform additional policy initiatives or process improvements. Third, while we speculate here on the possible reasons for few HIOs and hospitals reporting that they submit claims of potential information blocking to HHS, understanding their reasons for not submitting claims could be valuable, both to HHS and the public. Obtaining and acting on this information could improve actors’ confidence in the utility of the information-blocking claims submission system and lead to more claims being submitted, which would better reflect the totality of the behavior and challenges that actors face in accessing, exchanging, and using EHI.

### Limitations

Our study is subject to several limitations. First, as a survey-based study, the reported results reflect respondents’ reported views and may reflect misinterpretation of the information-blocking rules or some bias in what respondents choose to report. Our use of triangulation across different entity types should reflect different levels of familiarity with the rules and different sources of bias, potentially mitigating these concerns. Second, while we have pursued multiple perspectives on information blocking, other entities likely have different experiences. Additional information on their experiences would be necessary to develop a comprehensive view of the prevalence of potential information-blocking practices. Finally, as noted elsewhere, any determination that the observations of hospital and HIO leaders reflect information blocking would depend on an investigation of each specific set of facts and circumstances. We have therefore referred to the reported practices as “potential information blocking” throughout.

## Conclusion

Our data indicate that the prevalence of hospitals and HIOs experiencing incidents that they perceive to potentially be information blocking has decreased since the effective date of the Cures Act Final Rule. However, hospitals and HIOs still report significant frictions around accessing and sharing health information. The specific characteristics of those frictions depend on the role these entities play in the broader health ecosystem. Very few hospitals and HIOs said that they have reported potential information blocking to HHS, indicating that publicly reported data by ASTP likely represents a lower bound and potentially unrepresentative view of potential information-blocking practices that are occurring.
