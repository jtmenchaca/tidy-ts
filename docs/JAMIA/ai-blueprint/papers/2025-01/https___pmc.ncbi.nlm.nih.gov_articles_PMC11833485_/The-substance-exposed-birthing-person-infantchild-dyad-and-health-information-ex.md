## Abstract

### Objective

Timely access to data is needed to improve care for substance-exposed birthing persons and their infants, a significant public health problem in the United States. We examined the current state of birthing person and infant/child (dyad) data-sharing capabilities supported by health information exchange (HIE) standards and HIE network capabilities for data exchange to inform point-of-care needs assessment for the substance-exposed dyad.

### Material and Methods

A cross-map analysis was performed using a set of dyadic data elements focused on pediatric development and longitudinal supportive care for substance-exposed dyads (70 birthing person and 110 infant/child elements). Cross-mapping was conducted to identify definitional alignment to standardized data fields within national healthcare data exchange standards, the United States Core Data for Interoperability (USCDI) version 4 (v4) and Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR) release 4 (R4), and applicable structured vocabulary standards or terminology associated with USCDI. Subsequent survey analysis examined representative HIE network sharing capabilities, focusing on USCDI and FHIR usage.

### Results

91.11% of dyadic data elements cross-mapped to at least 1 USCDI v4 standardized data field (87.80% of those structured) and 88.89% to FHIR R4. 75% of the surveyed HIE networks reported supporting USCDI versions 1 or 2 and the capability to use FHIR, though demand is limited.

### Discussion

HIE of clinical and supportive care data for substance-exposed dyads is supported by current national standards, though limitations exist.

### Conclusion

These findings offer a dyadic-focused framework for electronic health record-centered data exchange to inform bedside care longitudinally across clinical touchpoints and population-level health.

**Keywords:** birthing person-infant dyad, electronic health record (EHR), health information exchange (HIE), substance exposure, United States Core Data for Interoperability (USCDI), fast healthcare interoperability resources (FHIR)

## Introduction

For over 2 decades, the United States opioid crisis has affected many populations, including birthing persons and their infants and children. From 2010 to 2017, the incidence of maternal opioid use disorder (OUD) increased by 4.6 and neonatal abstinence syndrome (NAS) by 3.3 per 1000 birth hospitalizations.[1](#ocae315-B1) NAS is postnatal withdrawal from exposure to prenatal opioids and other substances.[2](#ocae315-B2) Significant disparities have been shown in the rates of OUD and NAS across rural versus urban communities, as well as associations between social determinants of health and rates of NAS.[3](#ocae315-B3),[4](#ocae315-B4) Although medications for treatment of maternal OUD can increase the risk of neonatal withdrawal, they also reduce overdose deaths, preterm birth, and low birth weight.[5](#ocae315-B5) OUD, and more broadly encompassing substance use disorder (SUD), together with NAS, highlight a confluence of physiological, social, and environmental factors that distinctly affect birthing persons and their infants as a dyad. Given the inextricable link between the wellbeing of birthing persons and their infants, an emphasis on the dyad highlights important considerations pertinent to clinical care, public health, and research.

To better understand the needs of the birthing person and infant as a dyad, timely data must be accessible to inform bedside care, public health surveillance, and research trial design. To-date, a vast majority of public health knowledge on NAS has relied upon hospital administrative claims data—known for significant time lags.[6](#ocae315-B6) One dyad-specific characteristic is the need to link birthing person and infant records, a retrospective approach commonly using Medicaid claims with vital birth records.[7](#ocae315-B7) While this administrative approach has been demonstrated as effective, it also highlights the challenge of combining 2 disparate data sources to create a dyadic data set. Additional challenges pertain to privacy considerations necessary for the exchange of sensitive dyadic data given criminalization and barriers to care that pregnant and parenting persons with SUD face.[8](#ocae315-B8),[9](#ocae315-B9) Similarly, clinical research data on NAS has its limitations, primarily due to a preponderance of nonrandomized and uncontrolled studies, small sample sizes, and retrospective data.[10](#ocae315-B10) Upstream of clinical research and public health data is the need for real-time data at the bedside. For clinical touchpoints, electronic health record (EHR)-centered approaches offer opportunities to inform time-sensitive medical decision-making for the dyad.

Together with the 21st Century Cures Act, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services EHR Incentive Program, or Meaningful Use, accelerated the adoption and use of certified EHR technology and created the regulatory framework to support interoperability, the exchange, and use of electronic health information among health systems.[11](#ocae315-B11),[12](#ocae315-B12) Development of Health Information Exchange (HIE), or the sharing of EHR-centered data across organizations, has been an essential part of the roadmap created for the future of United States interoperability.[13](#ocae315-B13),[14](#ocae315-B14) For the dyad, HIE use affords not only a pathway to query pertinent data to inform patient encounters, but also an opportunity for longitudinal data access across geographic locations and essential clinical timepoints—including prenatal, birth, postpartum, infant, and child development. Leveraging this regulatory framework, the present analysis was designed to advance an understanding of the landscape of EHR-centered data exchange by determining: (1) the current state of birthing person and infant and child data-sharing capabilities included in standards supporting HIE and (2) HIE network capability to exchange dyadic data to inform the point-of-care needs assessment for the substance-exposed dyad.

## Methods

The basis for this analysis was a recommended set of data categories and associated individual data elements developed to support longitudinal needs assessment for the substance-exposed birthing person-infant/child dyad.[15](#ocae315-B15) The set was developed through a collaboration between the Department of Health and Human Services and national maternal-child health experts with a focus on clinical information routine to age-based pediatric encounters (infancy through adolescence) plus pertinent dyadic supportive care (dyadic data elements; 70 birthing person, 110 infant/child). See Supplement for data categories/elements.

First, the research team developed a cross-map to assess which dyadic data elements are supported within at least 1 standardized data field of 2 national healthcare data classification and exchange standards: (1) United States Core Data for Interoperability (USCDI), a standardized set of health data classes and elements which defines _what_ information is needed for nationwide HIE and required by the Office of the National Coordinator (ONC) for Health Information Technology (IT) Certification Criteria for EHRs, and (2) Health Level 7 (HL7) Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR), another standard providing the technical protocols and framework for _how_ data needs to be structured and exchanged to achieve nationwide interoperability and an integral part of the roadmap for the Trusted Exchange Framework Common AgreementSM (TEFCA), a 21st Century Cures Act requirement that outlines a common set of principles, terms, and conditions stipulating creation of a national network of HIEs.[16–20](#ocae315-B16)

Second, a focused cross-map to USCDI was developed to determine which dyadic data elements could be exchanged and discretely consumed by recipient EHRs using a common (structured) language to support point-of-care data visualization and clinical decision support. Accordingly, dyadic data elements were assessed and categorized as _structured_ if an applicable vocabulary or terminology standard (eg, RxNorm) was associated with the USCDI data element and labeled as _unstructured_ if no applicable vocabulary standard was associated with the USCDI data element. See Supplement for cross-mapping. Third, a survey analysis was developed to examine the current capability of HIE networks to support exchange of the dyadic data elements.

### Cross-mapping

Based on the dyadic data element’s specified purpose(s), represented as diagnosis, laboratory, medication, assessment, or combination thereof, each dyadic data element was individually assessed for cross-map to USCDI version 4 (v4) and FHIR release 4 (R4).[21](#ocae315-B21),[22](#ocae315-B22) Cross-mapping entailed an iterative process by 2 members of the research team consisting of: (1) assessment for definitional alignment between the dyadic data element’s purpose(s) and USCDI data elements and FHIR resources (2) independent iterative review of definitional matches; with confirmatory research team review to achieve agreement for any matches in question. Further, to assess whether dyadic data elements were supported as structured data fields within USCDI, cross-mapping to applicable vocabulary standards as defined by the USCDI data element specifications comprised of: International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10), Systemized Nomenclature of Medicine Clinical Terms (SNOMED CT), Logical Observation Identifiers, Names, and Codes (LOINC), and RxNorm.

### HIE network selection and survey

To determine HIE network capability to exchange dyadic data elements, an assessment was conducted to identify HIE platforms with greatest coverage across United States healthcare institutions—39 HIEs were identified. This included global, national, state, and regional HIE platforms with inclusion criteria (1) publicly available metrics for geographic coverage, (2) coverage by healthcare site and type, and (3) the ONC’s candidacy as a Qualified Health Information Network (QHIN; networks with technical capabilities and organizational attributes to connect health information nationwide under TEFCA). This information was manually curated from internet resources and publicly available HIE websites, with 13 networks meeting inclusion criteria. See [Supplement Appendix](#sup1) for full list of HIEs.

Utilizing a semi-structured interview, network leaders were interviewed between June and July 2023 with the specific aim to better understand EHR-centered data exchange for the substance-exposed dyad. Questions were focused on understanding the current state of adoption of data standards, as well as key challenges in HIE for the birthing person-infant/child dyad. The challenges explored included adoption of strategies to segment sensitive health information, such as information related to SUD, mental health disorders, reproductive health, sexually transmitted illnesses, and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), as well as strategies to accommodate the complexities of identity matching algorithms for infants given name changes (ie, birth name versus legal name) and the dirth of associated demographic information at birth required for matching algorithms. Interview questions included: (1) use of USCDI and FHIR data standards (and associated versions), (2) plans for adoption of national health IT certification privacy standard, Data Segmentation for Privacy (DS4P; a standard allowing consent-driven exchange of sensitive information, eg, substance use across health systems and providers), and (3) rules for infant name matching—a common challenge in HIE—including newborn names, hyphenated names, and name changes.[23](#ocae315-B23),[24](#ocae315-B24) See [Supplement Appendix](#sup1) for survey questions. Responses were analyzed using descriptive statistics.

## Results

### Cross-mapping

Considering the current United States regulatory framework for EHR data standards, dyadic data elements were cross-mapped to the most updated standards, USCDI v4 (draft v4 and finalized v4) and FHIR R4. The finalized USCDI v4 (published July 2023) includes substance use data elements not included in prior versions.[21](#ocae315-B21) Cross-mapping was graphically depicted using Sankey diagrams, which are typically applied to display flows between successive steps using edges, whose thickness is proportional to the flow quantity.

#### United States Core Data for Interoperability

One hundred and sixty-four of 180 (91.11%) dyadic data elements cross-mapped to at least 1 USCDI data element. Of these, 144 (87.8%) were supported within structured data fields. 66 of 70 (94.29%) birthing person and 98 of 110 (89.09%) child data elements cross-mapped to at least 1 USCDI data element.

Of the birthing person data elements, 37 of 37 (100%) clinical and 29 of 33 (87.88%) supportive care cross-mapped to at least 1 USCDI data element ([Figure 1A and B](#ocae315-F1)). Of these, 63 (95.45%) were supported within structured data fields (100% clinical and 89.66% supportive care).

#### Fast healthcare interoperability resources

One hundred and sixty of 180 (88.89%) dyadic data elements cross-mapped to at least 1 FHIR resource. 67 of 70 (95.71%) birthing person and 93 of 110 (84.55%) infant/child data elements cross-mapped to at least 1 FHIR resource (see [Supplement Appendix](#sup1)). Of the birthing person data elements, 36 of 37 (97.3%) clinical and 31 of 33 (93.94%) supportive care data elements cross-mapped to at least 1 FHIR resource. Of the infant/child data elements, 62 of 65 (95.38%) clinical and 31 of 45 (68.89%) supportive care cross-mapped to at least 1 FHIR resource.

### HIE network responses

Nine of 13 HIE networks responded via either written survey (3 networks; 33.3%) or semi-structured interview (6 networks; 66.7%) ([Table 1](#ocae315-T1)). Eight of 9 responses were included from entities identified as HIE networks and participate in data exchange, including 5 of the 6 candidate 2023 QHINs; one organization was excluded from further analysis as it functions as an HIE framework (not as an HIE). Key themes identified from network interviews include: (1) Usage of USCDI and FHIR; HIE networks (5 of 8, 62.5%) exchange data in USCDI versions 1 or 2 with plans to adopt future versions based on regulatory requirements. Although most (6 of 8, 75%) HIE networks can support data in the HL7 FHIR format and convert data obtained in other formats into the FHIR format, usage is limited given current demand from health systems. (2) Plans for adoption of certification privacy standards; Only one of the networks (12.5%) reported being in the process of implementing DS4P at the time of the survey/interviews. (3) Rules for patient matching; HIE networks (7 of 8, 87.5%) reported the ability to exchange newborn infant names, and 6 of 8 (75%) reported the ability to exchange and match hyphenated names.

## Discussion

Recent recommendations supporting a dyadic approach to care for substance-exposed birthing persons and their infants highlight the need for improving data collection for clinical, public health, and research purposes.[25–30](#ocae315-B25) At the state level, mandated NAS reporting has introduced opportunities for improving quantification of NAS incidence, informing program and service development for substance-exposed birthing people and infants.[31–34](#ocae315-B31) However, long-term data collection beyond initial case reporting remains a challenge. As a surveillance network, the Maternal and Infant Network to Understand Outcomes Associated with Medication for Opioid Use Disorder During Pregnancy collects longitudinal data on opioid-exposed pregnant person-infant dyads to improve outcomes.[35](#ocae315-B35) Moreover, the Data Modernization Initiative aims to advance public health surveillance and data by modernization of the health data infrastructure across federal and state public health settings.[36](#ocae315-B36) Upstream of public health surveillance and research, there are limitations in both access and timeliness of clinical and supportive needs data at the bedside. As presented here, a framework for point-of-care dyadic data exchange is a first step toward addressing this gap—essential for both patient- and population-level health.

While the present was a cross-map analysis of dyadic data elements to USCDI and FHIR, it is noteworthy that the former is required as part of national EHR certification criteria and the latter was found to have limited usage by HIE networks given current demand from health systems. For USCDI v4, 94.29% of birthing person and 89.09% of infant/child data elements referenced in this analysis cross-mapped to at least 1 standardized data field, supporting access at the point-of-care and electronic sharing across networks. This indicates that implementation of USCDI v4 can offer timely access to data pertinent for clinical decision-making; secondarily, it can inform the development of EHR-mediated tools for clinicians across inpatient and outpatient care settings. Specific applications are considerable but can include dyadic dashboards or applications embedded within the EHR (eg, Substitutable Medical Applications and Reusable Technologies \[SMART\] on FHIR applications) developed for point-of-care determination of clinical and supportive care needs coupled with referrals to programs and services for birthing person and child.[37](#ocae315-B37) Moreover, application of dyadic data elements supported within structured data fields can inform development of clinical algorithms and decision support for clinicians caring for the dyad.

Updates to national data standards, such as inclusion of substance use data indicate a shift in capabilities that better supports interoperability for dyadic data exchange across clinical touchpoints, healthcare institutions, and longitudinal timepoints. For clinicians caring for the dyad across different inpatient and outpatient settings, this can address data challenges in access to prenatal records pertinent to birthing person and neonate needs assessment. It is important to note that the gaps in USCDI and FHIR data standards cross-mapping to the dyadic data elements applied in this study mostly impacted supportive care dyadic data, such as data related to foster care and community and social supports, reflective of key social determinants of health influencing the care of the dyad. Given the increasing recognition of the influence of social factors on healthcare outcomes, including for the substance-exposed dyad, this reflects an important area for ongoing efforts to establish additional data and vocabulary standards to facilitate the capture and exchange of these data elements. While the dyadic-focused data elements used here are not all-encompassing, they present a framework for data access and exchange that can be used to develop more comprehensive data element sets.

Finally, this analysis is subject to multiple limitations and considerations that may impact HIE for the substance-exposed dyad and merit further research and exploration.

### HIE adoption

While interoperability among healthcare organizations has been increasing, HIE adoption has not been uniform across healthcare settings or geographic locations. Despite recent gains in data exchange among rural and smaller hospitals (under 100 beds), they lag behind their counterparts (urban/suburban location and medium/large hospitals >100 beds) with respect to interoperability.[38](#ocae315-B38),[39](#ocae315-B39) Hospitals engaging with patients from zip codes with a high social deprivation index—validated census-level data indicative of the relative deprivation of geographic regions—have also been found less likely to participate in interoperable exchange.[40](#ocae315-B40) This creates inequities in point-of-care data availability for clinicians managing patients with high social deprivation and in rural and smaller hospital settings.

### Technical limitations

While adoption of USCDI and FHIR standards and capabilities exist across most HIE organizations interviewed, there is variability in the extent of adoption and the specific version of standards used, with most planning to adopt USCDI version 3 now that the ONC’s Health Data, Technology, and Interoperability proposed rule—which raises the required USCDI version from 1 to 3—is finalized.[41](#ocae315-B41) Although this analysis’ focus on USCDI v4 was intended to optimize capture of substance use data, not available in previous USCDI versions, v4 implementation across HIE networks remains limited.[42](#ocae315-B42) Key data elements included in v4, health assessments related to alcohol and substance use, are particularly useful given restrictions in disclosures and access to substance use treatment records as stipulated by 42 CFR part 2 (regulatory restriction related to disclosing SUD records); and would provide needed information where SUD treatment records are not available (eg, for birthing persons not enrolled in a treatment program).[43](#ocae315-B43) Additionally, even for data elements that can be cross-mapped to a standardized data field, there is variability in documentation practices and concordance of codes used for the same data elements. Data elements may be included in the narrative text of a document, rather than in designated structured data fields, limiting the ability to exchange salient information in a structured fashion.[44](#ocae315-B44),[45](#ocae315-B45) This is particularly true for data elements related to social determinants of health and social and community supports, where the narrative text often provides greater details and richer information.[46](#ocae315-B46) While this analysis examined the availability of data standards and structured vocabulary standards that cross-map to and are available for the dyadic data elements for HIE, further studies would be needed to examine the actual frequency with which the data is exchanged in a codified manner versus included in narrative text in real world settings.

### Patient matching

Successful data exchange also depends on appropriately matching patients across healthcare settings, which can be particularly challenging for neonates. While most HIE networks use probabilistic patient matching algorithms to optimize correct matches, these algorithms are still far from perfect, with matching failures often due to lack of standardized data collection across institutions (eg, name conventions), changes in information over time (eg, names, addresses), or missing information.[47–51](#ocae315-B47) These challenges are compounded in neonates, where naming conventions vary and birth names are often replaced by legal names after hospital discharge, resulting in mismatches. Multiple births further exacerbate matching problems. Moreover, social security numbers, which can provide the highest weighted matching element for algorithms, are generally missing for neonates.[24](#ocae315-B24)

### Legal and ethical considerations

Given the risk for stigmatization and potential consequences including criminal and domestic proceedings related to parental custody, privacy considerations are paramount to discussions of data surrounding SUD patient records.[8](#ocae315-B8),[9](#ocae315-B9) As a result, legal and ethical considerations may limit the availability of desired data elements across HIE. In addition to regulatory restrictions related to disclosing SUD treatment records, other sensitive information (eg, birthing person’s mental health diagnosis and treatment, HIV or reproductive health data) may not be available due to institutional policies, state laws or patient request that select information not be shared, as is permitted by the privacy information blocking exception.[52](#ocae315-B52) Furthermore, individual consent to share sensitive information across HIE networks, eg, by leveraging the DS4P standard, would provide greater flexibility and a patient-centered approach to data-sharing, but, as reported by the HIEs we surveyed, consent-driven data segmentation and exchange of sensitive health information has not been widely adopted.[23](#ocae315-B23)

### EHR data presentation

Finally, accessing the dyadic data elements in a single view at the EHR point-of-care exposes additional challenges. Linking the birthing person and infant within an EHR and across HIE networks remains a functional limitation across most technologies, resulting in barriers in data availability to assist in optimal treatment decisions, identification of social needs, and resource allocation.[53](#ocae315-B53),[54](#ocae315-B54) Moreover, access to birthing person data through HIE platforms requires status as a registered patient (to allow for HIE queries), which is not the standard for clinics or institutions where only the infant is a patient.

Additional limitations merit further discussion. Although an iterative, confirmatory process was applied with the data cross mapping, there is subjectivity inherent to cross-mapping, with multiple possibilities for matching data elements to data fields, subject to researcher interpretation. Absence of data from all HIE networks identified for inclusion in the study may have resulted in selection bias, and potentially influenced findings. Moreover, although the data elements used here were developed to support longitudinal needs assessment for the substance-exposed dyad, they are not comprehensive; absence of other potentially relevant data elements may have influenced findings from the cross-map analysis. Lastly, considering the possibility of HIE network workflow updates, versions of USCDI and FHIR use reported here may have changed since this data collection.

## Conclusions

Despite multiple challenges identified, there is a significant foundational infrastructure for EHR-centered point-of-care access and exchange of dyadic data elements between healthcare systems in a standardized manner. These findings provide a framework for dyadic data access and exchange and create a particular case for the use of USCDI v4 as the recommended standard for implementation to ensure data inclusion around the substance-exposed dyad in exchanges more consistently across the United States. A key future direction is development of EHR-centered technical solutions to link the birthing person and infant dyad across HIE settings and the care continuum to optimize access to complete and relevant data for clinicians at the point-of-care.
