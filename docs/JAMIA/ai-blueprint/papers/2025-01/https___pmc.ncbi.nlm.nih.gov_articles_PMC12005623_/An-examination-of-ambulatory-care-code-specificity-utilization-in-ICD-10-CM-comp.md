## Abstract

### Objective

The ICD-10-CM classification system contains more specificity than its predecessor ICD-9-CM. A stated reason for transitioning to ICD-10-CM was to increase the availability of detailed data. This study aims to determine whether the increased specificity contained in ICD-10-CM is utilized in the ambulatory care setting and inform an evidence-based approach to evaluate ICD-11 content for implementation planning in the United States.

### Materials and Methods

Diagnosis codes and text descriptions were extracted from a 25% random sample of the IQVIA Ambulatory EMR—US database for 2014 (ICD-9-CM, _n_ = 14 327 155) and 2019 (ICD-10-CM, _n_ = 13 062 900). Code utilization data was analyzed for the total and unique number of codes. Frequencies and tests of significance determined the percentage of available codes utilized and the unspecified code rates for both code sets in each year.

### Results

Only 44.6% of available ICD-10-CM codes were used compared to 91.5% of available ICD-9-CM codes. Of the total codes used, 14.5% ICD-9-CM codes were unspecified, while 33.3% ICD-10-CM codes were unspecified.

### Discussion

Even though greater detail is available, a 108.5% increase in using unspecified codes with ICD-10-CM was found. The utilization data analyzed in this study does not support a rationale for the large increase in the number of codes in ICD-10-CM. New technologies and methods are likely needed to fully utilize detailed classification systems.

### Conclusion

These results help evaluate the content needed in the United States national ICD standard. This analysis of codes in the current ICD standard is important for ICD-11 evaluation, implementation, and use.

**Keywords:** ICD-10-CM, ICD-11, code specificity, code utilization

## Background and significance

The International Classification of Diseases (ICD) is the basis for “understanding diseases in ways that enable prevention, treatment, and the allocation of resources.”[1](#ocaf003-B1) ICD-10 was endorsed by the World Health Assembly (WHO) in 1990 and introduced in 1992.[2](#ocaf003-B2) Due to the clinical modifications needed for use in the United States, as well as the unique regulatory process, ICD-10-CM implementation was a long process occurring years after the initial ICD-10 publication.[3](#ocaf003-B3) ICD-10-CM was implemented in the United States on October 1, 2015. In 2019, ICD-10-CM contained 71 932 codes compared to 14 567 codes in ICD-9-CM in 2014.[4](#ocaf003-B4),[5](#ocaf003-B5)

ICD-11 was endorsed by the WHO in 2019, and the hope is that, in the United States, this edition will require fewer, if any, clinical modifications, thus speeding along its implementation.[1](#ocaf003-B1) ICD-11 incorporates advances in information technology, making it the first digitally-enabled edition of ICD. Unlike all prior versions, ICD-11 is much more than an organized list of codes. The innovative architecture of ICD-11 includes 3 key components: the Foundation Component, a Content Model, and Linearizations. The Content Model provides structure and content for the Foundation, such as relationships and definitions. The Foundation Component is the core of ICD-11 and includes all ICD entities, each with a unique identifier. Linearizations are a defined subset of entities derived from the Foundation for a particular statistical purpose, organized into categories, and assigned codes. In addition, ICD-11 uses a stem code as the foundational concept with the option to link additional stem or extension code(s) to reflect additional concept details. This feature is known as postcoordination.[6](#ocaf003-B6) An example of this is the detailed description of lung cancer. At the time of this writing, ICD-11 MMS includes a stem code and description “2C25.1, Small cell carcinoma of bronchus or lung.” Extension codes that could be used for this condition include “XK9J (Bilateral), XS4P (Stage II), and XA57M6 (Lung).” An example of a postcoordinated code cluster is: 2C25.1 &XK9J &XS4P &XA57M6 (bilateral small cell carcinoma of bronchus or lung, stage II in the lung).[7](#ocaf003-B7)

Despite these significant differences in the structure of ICD-11 versus ICD-10-CM, ICD-10-CM is being used as the gold standard against which ICD-11 is measured.[8–11](#ocaf003-B8) This is particularly true when evaluating the content coverage of ICD-11. As previously noted, ICD-10-CM contained 71 932 codes in 2019, all pre-coordinated, and many were combination codes. A combination code is one where multiple concepts are included in the code text description. In contrast, ICD-11 has few combination codes. Instead, postcoordination is used to capture expanded details. This makes content comparisons more complex. In addition, evidence is needed on whether the more detailed codes in ICD-10-CM have indeed resulted in the availability of more detailed data. The current investigation is important because it focuses on the frequency of use of ICD-10-CM diagnosis codes to determine whether the more specific codes are being utilized or if unspecified codes are being employed.

Ambulatory care data was used in this investigation because this setting is very commonly utilized for care.[12](#ocaf003-B12) In addition, this setting is where clinicians have the least support for coding from staff with formal coding training.

ICD-10-CM, when first designed, was to bring a greater degree of specificity to assigned codes for patient encounters. However, to facilitate implementation and allow users to become familiar with the increased specificity available, when ICD-10-CM was launched in 2015 the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid (CMS) granted a 1-year grace period. During this grace period, claims were not denied if the code was from the correct family. Implementation involved substituting ICD-10-CM codes where ICD-9-CM codes were used. Though the code tables were updated across systems, the user interface with ICD changed very little beyond presenting the new codes. After the implementation year, reported ICD-10-CM unspecified code rates ranged from 20% to 40% creating uncertainty on the extent to which the increased specificity in ICD-10-CM was adopted.[13](#ocaf003-B13) It’s also unclear whether ICD-10-CM was deployed in a manner that could facilitate the use of the increased specificity available.

A study on emergency department visits from the Veterans Health Administration (VHA) focused on the use of code specificity before and after the transition from ICD-9-CM to ICD-10-CM.[13](#ocaf003-B13) This study concentrated on coding disparity and specificity between 2014 and 2020, paying attention to demographics, diagnoses, and comorbid conditions. The most common conditions were back pain, bronchitis, chest pain, and abdominal pain. The use of specified codes for back pain was similar for ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM, 32% versus 31%. Similar results were found for bronchitis, while chest pain showed more specific codes used with ICD-9-CM (29%) than ICD-10-CM (18%). For abdominal pain ICD-10-CM used more specific codes 66% of the time compared to 35% for ICD-9-CM. Overall, the study found that for these encounters a small number of codes were used with a lack of application of the more detailed ICD-10-CM codes.[14](#ocaf003-B14)

Chronic conditions are significant to the patient’s medical history and important to track over time. In addition, chronic conditions are often reported with unspecified codes. One of the fears when transitioning from ICD-9-CM to ICD-10-CM was that certain chronic conditions would be reported in ICD-9-CM and not in ICD-10-CM due to a lack of a direct correlation between codes. A study conducted in conjunction with the Veterans Affairs (VA) healthcare system sought to examine the reporting of chronic conditions during the transition from ICD-9-CM to ICD-10-CM. The study results showed that the “prevalence rates were similar across all study years for most conditions,” but certain categories did show a rate change.[15](#ocaf003-B15) For example, the authors reported the incidence of Alzheimer’s disease increased by 0.2% between 2015 and 2016, whereas mental health and substance use disorders decreased during this time. The reasons for these variances include more specificity of ICD-10-CM mental health codes, the ability to capture the diagnosis with one ICD-10-CM code instead of multiple ICD-9-CM codes, and the omitting of secondary conditions when they are not the reason for the patient encounter.[14](#ocaf003-B14),[15](#ocaf003-B15)

## Objective

The objective of the current study was to help the United States explore the frequency of use of ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM codes available, as well as the subset of unspecified codes, in ambulatory care for morbidity reporting to inform ICD-11 content evaluation and implementation planning.

### Materials and methods

A 25% random sample of the IQVIA Ambulatory EMR—US database was chosen for this study, as previously mentioned, because most patient care is delivered in the ambulatory care environment.[12](#ocaf003-B12) IQVIA Ambulatory EMR—US is a collection of patient electronic medical records captured from interactions between a patient and their healthcare provider. Patient electronic medical records include patient demographics, problems and diagnoses, vitals, lab orders and results, procedures, and medications. The data is sourced from over 800 ambulatory practices covering more than 100 000 health care practitioners. It includes over 97 million patients since 2006, with an average history of 3 years.[16](#ocaf003-B16)

This study began with a review of the IQVIA data dictionary to determine which record types were relevant. For the study it was important to include the records containing ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM codes. Codes from 2014 (ICD-9-CM) and 2019 (ICD-10-CM) were chosen because 2014 is the last full year of ICD-9-CM use before ICD-10-CM was implemented in 2015. The year 2019 was chosen for the ICD-10-CM data because it was 4 years after implementation and was the last full year of data prior to the COVID pandemic.

The coded data was pulled from patient order and problem table data fields. These tables were chosen because both contained diagnosis codes. It was decided to focus only on the problem table as the order table reflects placed orders for diagnostic testing, and so is skewed towards unspecified. The problem table contains multiple different variables, including one titled ICD 10 Code and one titled ICD 9 code. These are both described as the respective diagnosis code for a patient problem, with no further metadata describing the codes as primary, secondary, or supplementary codes. Other variables found in this table are CPT Code, Problem ID, Problem Name, Problem Onset, and so on. The data was analyzed using Tableau, a data analytics and visualization application as well as Excel and SPSS Version 26 for statistical tests.[17](#ocaf003-B17) A code was considered unspecified if one of the terms unspecified or not otherwise specified (NOS) was present in the code description.

### ICD-10-CM

The total number of problems in 2019 that utilized ICD-10-CM codes was 13 062 900. ICD-10-CM in 2019 had 71 932 codes available to be used. The number of unique ICD-10-CM codes used in the problems was 32 108, or 44.6% of available codes. After running calculations for total and unique codes, the frequency of ICD-10-CM codes were obtained. The frequency data was used to identify which ICD-10-CM codes were used most frequently and determine the number of unspecified codes selected. A careful review of the individual ICD-10-CM codes revealed invalid codes (_n_ = 538). Any codes that were not valid in the 2019 dataset were removed from further analyses. The published version from the National Center for Health Statistics, the ICD Coordinating Center, was employed to determine the valid codes. To show the most frequent codes, the ICD-10-CM codes were sorted in Tableau in descending order.

The last step in analyzing the ICD-10-CM data was to calculate the frequency of unspecified codes. This unspecified code rate is determined by adding the total of all codes that have unspecified or NOS in their descriptions and dividing that by the total number of codes for that year.[13](#ocaf003-B13) Both the unspecified and NOS codes were totaled together, and this number was divided by the total number of ICD-10-CM codes for 2019 to calculate the unspecified code rate.

### ICD-9-CM

In 2014 the ICD-9-CM system contained a total of 14 567 codes. Of these, a total of 13 334, or 91.5%, were utilized in the problems. The 2014 dataset of ICD-9-CM codes was examined to identify null, invalid, or partial codes. All of those determined not to be valid in 2014 (_n_ = 95) were excluded from further analysis. The published version from the National Center for Health Statistics, the ICD Coordinating Center, was utilized to determine valid codes.

Like the ICD-10-CM data analysis, the last step in analyzing the ICD-9-CM data was to calculate the unspecified code rate. As was done for the ICD-10-CM data, the unspecified and NOS ICD-9-CM codes were totaled together. The total was divided by the total number of ICD-9-CM codes for 2014 to obtain an unspecified code rate.

Pearson’s chi-square tests were employed to determine whether the differences in ICD-9-CM code utilization compared to ICD-10-CM code utilization were statistically significant. The 2-proportion z-test was used to determine whether the proportion of unspecified codes in each version, as well as the proportion of unspecified unique codes used were statistically significant.

## Discussion

The results of this study are beneficial in helping the United States understand the available data represented by the current ICD-10-CM code set. To evaluate ICD-11 content as fit for use or offering comprehensive coverage, it’s important to understand whether the more detailed codes in ICD-10-CM have indeed resulted in the availability of more detailed data. This is an important starting point to determine what the United States needs in the ICD standard going forward.

As seen in [Table 4](#ocaf003-T4), the total number of records using the ICD-9-CM data set in 2014 (14 327 155) is slightly higher than the total for ICD-10-CM in the 2019 data set (13 062 900). This may reflect differences in the patient populations from 2014 to 2019 but is more likely a reflection of the increased number of combination (precoordinated) codes available in the ICD-10-CM code set. [Table 1](#ocaf003-T1) shows that there are more unique ICD-10-CM codes used than unique ICD-9-CM codes used. This is expected as there are more codes in the ICD-10-CM code set to choose from. Further analysis of the types of codes utilized or not utilized in each code set can provide important implications for the United States in considering clinical classification development, implementation, and use.

As previously noted, the ICD-10-CM code set contains 71 932 codes compared to 14 567 codes in ICD-9-CM.[4](#ocaf003-B4),[5](#ocaf003-B5) However, the utilization data analyzed in this study do not support a rationale for the large increase in the number of codes in the ICD-10-CM. As shown in [Table 1](#ocaf003-T1), 91.5% of the codes available in the ICD-9-CM code set were included in the 2014 data set at least once. In comparison, the proportion of ICD-10-CM codes used at least once in the 2019 data set was only 44.6%. While the number of unique codes utilized for ICD-10-CM is larger than that of ICD-9-CM, so is the significant use of unique unspecified codes. While some of the descriptions may be richer, there is no evidence to suggest that an extremely large number of ICD-10-CM codes is necessary in the ambulatory care context, given that more than half were not used in this large dataset. This may indicate that having more codes available in the code set does not necessarily translate to an increase in the variety of codes used. Other factors, such as the setting in which they are used and the technology, user interface, and training to manage the number of codes may be important.

Despite the specificity that ICD-10-CM codes offer, unspecified codes are frequently employed. The study of emergency department visits from the VHA by Grasso et al. focused on the use of code specificity before and after the transition from ICD-9-CM to ICD-10-CM.[14](#ocaf003-B14) The most common reasons for emergency department encounters were back pain, bronchitis, chest pain, and abdominal pain. For 3 out of 4 of these common reasons (the exception being abdominal pain), the unspecified code was applied most frequently to reflect these encounters. The code utilization data in the current analysis shows that back pain and acute bronchitis had a high unspecified code rate for ICD-9-CM while chest pain and abdominal pain had a high unspecified code rate for ICD-10-CM.

[Table 3](#ocaf003-T3) presents the top 10 most frequent unspecified conditions in the data sets. The variety of conditions in these tables, as well as the low percent use for any one code indicates that unspecified code use is not concentrated on any particular code. Rather, unspecified codes were/are used throughout both code sets. The conditions coded as unspecified in ICD-9-CM compared to ICD-10-CM in these same tables are surprisingly different, only hyperlipidemia and hypothyroidism appeared in both the top 10 for ICD-9-CM and for ICD-10-CM. This may be partially due to changes in code descriptions between the code sets. For example, the number one unspecified ICD-9-CM code is 401.9 (Unspecified essential hypertension). The most closely related code in ICD-10-CM (I10, Essential \[primary\] hypertension) does not necessarily include more clinical detail. Still, it is no longer described as “unspecified” and therefore is not included in the unspecified code rate. Similarly, the ICD-9-CM code, 729.5 (Pain in limb) is not classified as an unspecified code for this analysis, while the ICD-10-CM code M79.603 (Pain in arm, unspecified) is included. The use of unspecified codes could benefit from further analysis in many ways. For example, one of the most used ICD-9-CM codes was otitis media, unspecified, with 34 717 instances. While this condition did not occur in the top 10 for ICD-10-CM unspecified codes, further investigation reveals this is primarily due to the use of laterality. Otitis media, unspecified, left ear (H66.92) was used 9607 times, while the code for the unspecified ear (H66.90) was used 8660 times, the code for right ear (H66.91) was used 8050 times, and the code for bilateral (H66.93) was used 6400 times for a grand total of 32 717 otitis media, unspecified codes in ICD-10-CM. Of the top 10 codes used for otitis media, only 2 did not include the unspecified term. These were acute suppurative OM with spontaneous rupture of ear drum, right (3784) and left ear (3621), respectively. Specificity is a complex concept, however, the significant increase from ICD-9-CM to ICD-10-CM raises important questions regarding the use of unspecified codes.

[Table 4](#ocaf003-T4) presents the unspecified code rates found in each data set. The unspecified code rate for ICD-10-CM is 33.3%, or 18.8% higher than the unspecified code rate for ICD-9-CM (14.5%). Based on this data, though increased specificity is available in ICD-10-CM, it does not appear that it is being utilized to any large extent. It is possible that how the code set is deployed may impact what codes are utilized. Though more codes are available in ICD-10-CM, if they are simply presented in the same way, in a longer pick list, that may not be particularly useful in and of itself. Only 44.6% of the ICD-10-CM codes were reported at least once in the 2019 dataset. That means more than half of the ICD-10-CM codes available were not used at all in the year. This may be related to the provider’s electronic health record (EHR) interface that presents the diagnosis codes for the provider to select. If this is the case, any future ICD implementation, including ICD-11, will require improved technology to ensure the entire code set can be used more readily. Expecting providers to search for additional details that may be available in an enhanced code set is not sustainable, as it would only increase the provider’s burden. Innovative technology solutions, such as artificial intelligence (AI), natural language processing (NLP), or others that suggest detailed ICD codes based on documentation may be required to resolve the lack of specificity in coded data. More research is needed to better understand how code set deployment and technology affect ICD coded data specificity. In the meantime, based on the utilization data in this study, there is scant evidence that ICD-10-CM utilization provides the details the United States healthcare system believes it needs. Specifically, the increased specificity in ICD-10-CM is not fully utilized in the ambulatory care setting.

### Limitations

There are limitations to this study. It was conducted using a 25% random sample of data in 1 database collected from the ambulatory care setting. Codes may be used in other care settings that may never be used in ambulatory care, which could affect the interpretation of the utilization results. Additionally, it is unknown whether the ambulatory settings that contributed to the database support research that drives the utilization of certain codes, such as those for rare diseases. The method selected for identifying unspecified codes, using only the words “unspecified” and “NOS” likely resulted in some unspecified codes, using other descriptors such as “other specified,” being missed. Further, the analysis was limited to 2 calendar years. It is noted that familiarity with the coding system may have played a role as ICD-9-CM had been used for decades, whereas ICD-10-CM had only been used for 4 years.

A significant limitation of this study includes the use of unspecified codes as an indicator of lack of detailed information. There are known and widely recognized exceptions to this, including the fact that some unspecified codes have details equivalent to more specified codes. This is the situation for essential hypertension. Further, some unspecified codes may have more details than other codes lacking the unspecified term. This occurs with the descriptions of “pain in arm, unspecified” and “pain in limb.” The code for the first phrase was counted for this study, while the second was not.

With the implementation of ICD-11 MMS proceeding in other countries,[18](#ocaf003-B18) there are many opportunities and a pressing need for further research related to the use of ICD-11 and any linearization in the United States. As described in the limitations, this study was limited to EHR data from the ambulatory care setting. The results for inpatient care code use may be different.

## Conclusion

As we prepare for ICD-11 and its implementation, our results demonstrate the importance of comprehensively examining what is and is not needed in the ICD standard in the United States. The transition from ICD-9-CM to ICD-10-CM brought with it the promise of increased specificity. This specificity was supposed to give a more accurate representation of patient encounters. Yet, 8 years after its implementation, only 44.6% of the ICD-10-CM codes are used, and unspecified codes continue to be used frequently in the ambulatory setting. The patient data set used for this study demonstrated that 33.3% of ICD-10-CM codes used in a large ambulatory care database for 2019 were unspecified. This raises the question of whether ICD-10-CM is the most appropriate gold standard when planning for ICD-11 implementation. Additionally, further research is needed to better understand how the presentation of ICD codes in today’s ambulatory EHR systems impacts what codes are utilized. New methods, approaches, or innovative technologies may be needed to fully utilize the details available in the standard code set, regardless of the ICD version.
