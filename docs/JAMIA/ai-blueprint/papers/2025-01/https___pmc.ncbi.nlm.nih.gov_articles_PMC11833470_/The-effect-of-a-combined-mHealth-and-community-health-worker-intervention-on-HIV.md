## Abstract

### Objective

To identify demographic, social, and clinical factors associated with HIV self-management and evaluate whether the CHAMPS intervention is associated with changes in an individual’s HIV self-management.

### Method

This study was a secondary data analysis from a randomized controlled trial evaluating the effects of the CHAMPS, a mHealth intervention with community health worker sessions, on HIV self-management in New York City (NYC) and Birmingham. Group comparisons and linear regression analyses identified demographic, social, and clinical factors associated with HIV self-management. We calculated interactions between groups (CHAMPS intervention and standard of care) over time (6 and 12 months) following the baseline observation, indicating a difference in the outcome scores from baseline to each time across groups.

### Results

Our findings indicate that missing medical appointments, uncertainty about accessing care, and lack of adherence to antiretroviral therapy are associated with lower HIV self-management. For the NYC site, the CHAMPS showed a statistically significant positive effect on daily HIV self-management (estimate = 0.149, SE = 0.069, 95% CI \[0.018 to 0.289\]). However, no significant effects were observed for social support or the chronic nature of HIV self-management. At the Birmingham site, the CHAMPS did not yield statistically significant effects on HIV self-management outcomes.

### Discussion

Our study suggests that CHAMPS intervention enhances daily self-management activities for people with HIV at the NYC site, indicating a promising improvement in routine HIV care.

### Conclusion

Further research is necessary to explore how various factors influence HIV self-management over time across different regions.

**Keywords:** HIV/AIDS, sexual minority, mHealth, community health worker, self-management, public health informatics

## Introduction

Antiretroviral therapy (ART) has transformed the treatment for people with HIV (PWH) by suppressing HIV replication, which enhances individual health and survival while also reducing HIV transmissibility to sexual partners.[1](#ocae322-B1),[2](#ocae322-B2) Despite these important benefits and its broad availability in the United States (US), achieving optimal ART adherence (defined as ≥95%)[3–5](#ocae322-B3) remains a significant challenge for PWH, with substantial variability in health outcomes across different regions.[6–8](#ocae322-B6) For instance, ART adherence issues are particularly pronounced in the South, which accounts for 52% (18 728) of new HIV cases annually.[9](#ocae322-B9) In 2023, it was estimated that only 61% of PWH in Alabama achieved viral suppression (<200 copies/mL).[10](#ocae322-B10) Similarly, in New York City (NYC), which includes 82% of all PWH in the state,[11](#ocae322-B11) 79% were virally suppressed, meaning they had undetectable viral load measurements.[12](#ocae322-B12) These disparities highlight the complexities of managing HIV within a fragmented healthcare system and underscore the challenges faced by underserved and marginalized populations.[13](#ocae322-B13) Consequently, the US government prioritized the State of Alabama (AL) and four NYC-area counties in New York State (study site locations) among the regions most affected by HIV infection as part of the Ending the HIV Epidemic Plan for America.[14](#ocae322-B14) This plan aims to reduce new HIV infections by 75% by 2025, and by 90% by 2030, focusing on four pillars: (1) Diagnose; (2) Treat; (3) Prevent; and (4) Respond.[15](#ocae322-B15)

In response to these challenges, our study introduces the CHAMPS intervention, a novel mobile health (mHealth) application (app) combined with community health worker (CHW) support designed to enhance ART adherence and assist PWH in managing their health effectively. Detailed information can be found in this protocol study.[13](#ocae322-B13)

Self-management of chronic conditions involves individuals' daily decisions and behaviors to manage their illness, including adhering to medical treatments, monitoring symptoms, taking their medication, and engaging with healthcare providers.[16–18](#ocae322-B16) Nonetheless, HIV self-management can be particularly challenging due to the disease stigma, disparities in education, accessibility to healthcare resources, and health outcomes.[17](#ocae322-B17) Recognizing the need for a valid and reliable tool to measure HIV self-management, Webel et al. developed and psychometrically tested a scale designed to assess the various aspects of HIV self-management behavior. This tool assists in measuring daily self-management decisions and supports interventions to enhance self-management among PWH.[17](#ocae322-B17)

In order to improve self-management behaviors in this population, the CHAMPS intervention leverages technology and personalized support, aiming to equip PWH with the assistance necessary to optimize their medication adherence and overall health management. This approach seeks to improve individual health outcomes and align with the broader public health goal of reducing HIV transmission.

## Objectives

This study aimed to identify demographic, social, and clinical factors associated with HIV self-management and evaluate whether the CHAMPS intervention is associated with changes in an individual’s HIV self-management.

## Methods

### Design

The study was a secondary data analysis of a randomized controlled trial (RCT) collected from May 2021 to May 2023 to evaluate the effect of the CHAMPS intervention on HIV self-management at two geographic locations, NYC (NY) and Birmingham (AL).

### Participants

Eligible participants were individuals (1) able to speak, read, and write in English or Spanish (NYC site only); (2) aged ≥18 years; (3) open to being assigned to any intervention arm; (4) diagnosed with HIV ≥ 6 months; (5) having an HIV-1 RNA level ≥200 copies/mL, or at least one “no-show” visit (ie, missing a scheduled HIV primary care visit) in the past 12 months, or reporting being virally unsuppressed in the past 12 months; (6) owning a smartphone; and (7) with the ability and willingness to provide informed consent for study participation and consent for access to medical records.[13](#ocae322-B13) The exclusion criteria were (1) residing in a nursing home, prison, and/or receiving in-patient psychiatric care at the time of enrollment; (2) having a terminal illness with a life expectancy <6 months; (3) planning to move out of the area in the next 12 months; and/or (4) participating in a study that targets viral suppression for PWH.[13](#ocae322-B13)

### CHAMPS intervention

The intervention group received the CHAMPS intervention, which combines the CleverCap pill bottle with WiseApp and CHW sessions, allowing participants to self-monitor and manage their medication adherence. The CleverCap pill bottle dispenses the prescribed medication and interfaces with WiseApp for real-time tracking ([Figure 1](#ocae322-F1)). In addition, the intervention includes CHW sessions, which focus on themes such as health literacy, re-engagement in care, medication adherence, support services, and HIV disclosure. In contrast, the control group received the standard of care at each site, including clinical care, mental health, and other site-specific social services as needed.[13](#ocae322-B13)

### HIV self-management

For this analysis, the primary outcome is HIV self-management, assessed through a validated 20-item questionnaire divided into three domains: (1) daily self-management health practices (items 1-12), (2) social support and HIV self-management (items 13-15), and (3) chronic nature of HIV self-management (items 16-20) ([Table S1](#sup1)).[17](#ocae322-B17) Responses are measured on a 3-point Likert Scale where, “1” suggests the item never occurs, “2” indicates it occurs some of the time, and “3” denotes it always occurs.[17](#ocae322-B17) HIV self-management is a continuous variable based on the average of responses to the questionnaire, with higher scores reflecting greater HIV self-management.

### Statistical analysis

To address Aim 1, which was to identify demographic, social, and clinical factors associated with HIV self-management, we used linear regression analysis. For Aim 2, which was to evaluate whether the CHAMPS intervention is associated with changes in an individual’s HIV self-management, we used linear mixed-effect regression models with a random intercept to account for individuals. These models compare outcome changes over time (baseline, 6 months, and 12 months), covering periods before and after the intervention between CHAMPS (intervention group) and standard-of-care (control group). Diagnostic plots were examined to assess the validity of the homoscedasticity assumption, focusing on the standardized residual against the fitted value.

We employed the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression, which performs variable selection and regularization to enhance the prediction accuracy of the statistical model. In this study, LASSO was used to identify a parsimonious subset of the variables associated with the outcome of interest, which, in this case, was the effect of the CHAMPS intervention on HIV self-management. By applying LASSO, we identified and selected covariates that minimized prediction error. This approach allowed us to build an efficient model, reducing the risk of overfitting. Sample description and models were adjusted for participant characteristics, such as race, gender identity, sexual orientation, employment status, barriers to HIV care (1. Missed HIV care appointment, 2. Unsure of where to obtain HIV care, 3. Distrust of doctors/the medical system, 4. Did not have a phone to make an HIV care appointment**)**, AIDS diagnosis (Yes), antiretroviral treatment adherence (No), years since of HIV diagnosis, and social engagement (1. Get together with friends, family, or partner to relax and have fun, 2. Talk with friends, family, or partner about things that were very personal or private). Data are presented as mean (SD) for normally distributed variables. Dichotomous variables are expressed as absolute numbers and percentages. Statistical analyses were performed in RStudio software version 4.2.1 (R Foundation), and 2-sided _P < .05_ indicated statistical significance.

### Data analysis by geographic site

Different geographic locations may exhibit variations in HIV incidence, public health strategies, HIV/STD awareness, and accessibility to healthcare services. To account for these geographic differences and their potential impact on the effectiveness of the CHAMPS intervention, we conducted separate analyses for our two study sites: NYC (NY) and Birmingham (AL).

In our analysis, we calculated standardized mean differences (SMDs) across NYC and Birmingham sites to assess the magnitude of differences between groups. We interpreted the SMD values as follows: SMD of 0.1 or less indicates no meaningful difference, 0.2-0.4 represents a small difference, 0.5-0.7 reflects a medium difference, and 0.8 or greater indicates a large difference. For covariates with SMD indicating differences between the NYC and Birmingham cohorts, we explored interactions between the CHAMPS intervention and these site-specific covariates. The triple interaction term was used to assess how the effect of the CHAMPS intervention on HIV self-management varied based on these covariates (eg, race, years since HIV diagnosis, and AIDS diagnosis) post-intervention, and depending on whether the participants received the intervention. A non-significant triple interaction term would indicate that the CHAMPS intervention had no differential effect based on these site-specific factors. For the years since the HIV diagnosis variable, we converted this continuous variable into a dichotomous variable, categorizing participants based on whether they had been diagnosed after 2004 using the median diagnosis year (2004) as the cutoff point.

### Factors associated with HIV self-management

Our findings indicate that individuals self-identified as Black have higher HIV self-management compared to non-Black individuals (estimate = 0.200, 95% CI \[0.127 to 0.273\]). We also found that social engagement is positively associated with HIV self-management. Specifically, talking about personal or private matters (estimate = 0.101, 95% CI \[0.061 to 0.141\]) and getting together for leisure activities, such as relaxation and fun (estimate = 0.092, 95% CI \[0.015 to 0.168\]) are associated with increased HIV self-management scores. These findings suggest the important role social support and meaningful interactions play in effective HIV management.

On the other hand, individuals who reported missing HIV care appointments (estimate = −0.233, 95% CI \[−0.372 to −0.095\]) and those who are unsure where to obtain HIV care as barriers to care (estimate = −0.725, 95% CI \[−1.061 to −0.389\]) are associated with lower self-management. In addition, another factor contributing to lower HIV self-management is a lack of adherence to ART (estimate = −0.349, 95% CI \[−0.521 to −0.177\]), suggesting the importance of medical assistance and treatment adherence for effective HIV self-management ([Figure 2](#ocae322-F2)).

### The effect of CHAMPS intervention on HIV self-management

Site 1 NYC (_n_ = 145): For the daily HIV self-management, a statistically significant positive effect was observed post-intervention (estimate = 0.149, SE = 0.069, 95% CI \[0.018 to 0.289\]) after accounting for covariates ([Table 2](#ocae322-T2)). The HIV self-management score changes from pre- to post-intervention were significantly different between the two groups. The difference in score change was 0.149. These results suggest that CHAMPS intervention improved daily management practices among PWH, which could be attributed to the effective utilization of technological tools such as the CleverCap pill bottle and WiseApp with CHW sessions, fostering better medication adherence routines. However, the estimated coefficients for social support score (estimate = 0.163, SE = 0.113, 95% CI \[−0.060 to 0.380\]) and chronic nature of HIV self-management score (estimate = 0.093, SE = 0.074, 95% CI \[−0.049 to 0.239\]) were not statistically significant post-intervention.

Disparity between NYC and Birmingham sites: Participant characteristics across the two sites may have varied and influenced the CHAMPS intervention’s effectiveness on HIV self-management. While the sites are similar in many characteristics, there are notable differences in race, HIV diagnosis, and the year since HIV diagnosis ([Table S2](#sup1)).

We found a higher proportion of participants in Birmingham identify as Black (81%) compared to NYC (69%, SMD = 0.270). However, the CHAMPS intervention does not appear to have a differential impact on HIV self-management for Black participants compared to those non-Blacks (estimate = −0.146, 95% CI \[−0.462 to 0.169\]) ([Table S3](#sup1)).

Regarding the mean year of HIV diagnosis between the two study sites, we found participants in NYC were diagnosed earlier (2001) compared to those in Birmingham (2007, SMD = 0.516) ([Table S2](#sup1)). Despite this difference, the CHAMPS intervention’s effectiveness revealed no significant interaction between the intervention and the time since HIV diagnosis (estimate = 0.143, 95%CI \[−0.118 to 0.403\]), suggesting that the intervention’s impact was comparable across participants ([Table S4](#sup1)). Disease progression also differed between sites, with 51% of NYC participants having an AIDS diagnosis compared to 20% in Birmingham (SMD = 0.681). However, the interaction between the CHAMPS intervention and an AIDS diagnosis was not significant (estimate = −0.011, 95% CI \[−0.283 to 0.261\]), suggesting that the intervention’s impact on HIV self-management is consistent across participants, regardless of disease progression ([Table S5](#sup1)).

To validate our statistical analysis, we analyzed whether heteroskedasticity might affect the standard errors reported in [Tables 2](#ocae322-T2) and [3](#ocae322-T3). Diagnostic plots of standardized residuals against fitted values for both NYC and Birmingham sites were examined to assess the adequacy of the model’s fit and evaluate whether the key assumptions of linear mixed-effects models were met ([Figures S1](#sup1) and [S2](#sup1)).

The residuals displayed a random scatter around the zero line without noticeable curvature or systematic patterns, suggesting that the assumption of linearity between covariates, including the CHAMPS intervention and the HIV self-management outcome, was satisfied. In addition, the spread of residuals appeared consistent across fitted values, supporting the validity of the homoscedasticity assumption. The diagnostic plots did not visually show significant outliers or influential points ([Figures S1](#sup1) and [S2](#sup1)). These results suggest that the models support the validity of the statistical inferences drawn from them.

## Discussion

Our study identified that participants' exposure to the CHAMPS intervention in NYC had significant improvement in daily HIV self-management practices. This suggests that the integration of technological tools with programmed reminders to track ART adherence is an important component of effective HIV management. Previous research in the US has demonstrated that technology-based interventions for HIV medication adherence are promising and can significantly enhance self-care in PWH, especially those at risk or with a history of non-adherence.[16](#ocae322-B16),[19–22](#ocae322-B19) The CHAMPS intervention, featuring the CleverCap pill bottle and WiseApp combined with CHW sessions, is targeted to significantly enhance participant benefits. This enhancement arises primarily from the app’s functionality, which enables users to manage their ART effectively. It provides notifications and reminders that help users maintain their medication routines. The app also offers visualizations that facilitate easy medication adherence tracking over time, allowing users to quickly identify trends and make necessary adjustments, ultimately leading to more efficient and higher-quality care. In contrast, the control group does not have these benefits.

The effect of CHAMPS on social support and the chronic nature of HIV self-management was not statistically significant for the NYC site. A potential interpretation is that the CHAMPS intervention was designed to enhance ART adherence through a mHealth intervention and CHW session and concentrate primarily on the individual’s interaction with their medication regimen via reminders, tracking, and educational content. Therefore, these tools might not adequately address the complex social needs for long-term HIV management, such as integrating health management into broader life contexts. In addition, the difficulty in showing the significant impact of the CHAMPS intervention on social support and the chronic nature of HIV self-management could also be attributed to the timeframe of the study. Managing chronic conditions and developing social support systems are processes that usually unfold over extended periods, potentially beyond the 12-month study period, resulting in changes in these areas that are difficult to detect within a shorter timeframe. Further research could explore the multifaceted needs of living with HIV in addition to medication adherence, such as incorporating counseling, community-building activities, or longer-term CHW engagement strategies to enhance HIV management outcomes.

At the Birmingham site, the intervention showed no significant effect on any of the HIV self-management outcomes. While there were notable disparities between the Birmingham and NYC cohorts, such as race, years since HIV diagnosis, and AIDS diagnosis, these differences did not significantly moderate the effectiveness of the CHAMPS intervention. The disparity in intervention effectiveness between the sites may be attributed to other factors, such as local cultural and social contexts, which were not directly measured in this study.

Our study also demonstrates different factors influencing the overall self-management of PWH. Missing appointments and uncertainties about where to access HIV care are significantly associated with decreased HIV self-management. These factors typically represent substantial barriers to healthcare access and are consistent with previous studies.[22](#ocae322-B22),[23](#ocae322-B23) These findings indicate the importance of healthcare systems to ensure that patients have consistent access to clinics and are well-informed about where and how to access HIV care services. Support should also facilitate adherence to treatment regimens, assist with managing clinic appointment schedules, and work on enhancing stigma and discrimination awareness.[22](#ocae322-B22)

In addition, our analysis indicates that a lack of adherence to ART is associated with decreased HIV self-management. These findings are consistent with other studies,[16](#ocae322-B16),[24](#ocae322-B24),[25](#ocae322-B25) which emphasize the importance of consistent medical follow-up and adherence to treatment protocols as fundamental components of successful HIV management. For instance, regular interaction with healthcare providers might ensure that patients receive the necessary guidance, support, and adjustments to their treatment plans. The continuity of care addresses barriers to medication adherence, such as side effects or regimen complexity, and encourages a supportive environment for patients. Furthermore, consistent ART adherence is essential for maintaining viral suppression and preventing the progression of the disease, as well as reducing the risk of HIV transmission. Thus, ensuring that patients have reliable access to healthcare providers and the support needed to adhere to ART protocols is important for improving HIV self-management and overall health outcomes.

Future interventions should combine medication adherence with psychosocial support, which is essential for thorough HIV management. Moreover, tailoring these interventions to the specific needs and circumstances of different populations could improve their effectiveness. For instance, adjustments may be necessary for interventions at the Birmingham site to align with the unique cultural and social dynamics of the area.

Limitations should be acknowledged. The HIV self-management outcomes were assessed using validated patient self-report measures, while reliable, are subject to the biases associated with self-reported data. In addition, the 12-month intervention may not have been sufficient to observe changes in some aspects of HIV self-management, particularly those that are more complex and multifaceted, such as social support and the chronic nature of HIV self-management. It is important to note that our analysis did not incorporate neighborhood-level factors due to a lack of detailed geographical data in the current study (eg, address or zip code). These factors could potentially explain variations in HIV self-management outcomes and the effectiveness of the CHAMPS intervention across Birmingham and NYC sites. Future research should include these contextual factors to provide a more comprehensive understanding of intervention effectiveness across different geographical and policy landscapes. Despite these limitations, this study offers meaningful applications regarding demographic, social, and clinical factors associated with HIV self-management. Moreover, the positive outcomes observed in the New York cohort post-intervention underscore the potential of mHealth interventions combined with CHW support in enhancing HIV self-management practices.

## Conclusion

Our findings suggest that participating in the CHAMPS intervention leads to benefits in daily self-management activities for PWH in NYC. While the intervention's impact on social support and the chronic nature of HIV self-management outcomes were not statistically significant for this sample, the CHAMPS intervention’s ability to improve routine self-management practices shows a promising improvement for HIV care. Further research is needed to examine how different factors influence HIV self-management over time across different geographic regions. Integration of the CHAMPS intervention into standard care may assist PWH in improving their HIV self-management.
